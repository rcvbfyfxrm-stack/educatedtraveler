I · Why this exists

Everything worth doing with your hands is still taught somewhere, by someone.

Almost none of it is written down in a way you can act on. The people who could tell you are working, not publishing.

So it stays a rumour, and another year goes.

So I keep a record: where each craft is still taught, and by whom.

A

Arnaud

Founder · Every skill here, researched in depth by me

I grew up in the Jura — about as far from the sea as you can get in France. I left school at 18 to find out what the world was really about, and I've been crossing it by sea and by land ever since — fifteen years of that, ten of them cooking on yachts, learning from the masters I met along the way. Here is some of it.

I · The beginning

So I went.

Everyone expected university and a career. I finished a science baccalauréate, worked factory shifts until I had two thousand euros, and flew to Melbourne instead.

II · The kitchens

Every kitchen a classroom.

It started in a Darwin dishpit, the day the chef ran short-handed and put a knife in my hand. The Michelin star came years later. Every chef a teacher.

III · The sea

More than fifty thousand nautical miles behind me.

First classes in Sydney Bay, a place on regatta crews by asking at the dock, then oceans. The sea cares about judgment, not a résumé.

IV · The depths

The deeper I went, the quieter it got.

Freediving Mexican cenotes and the open blue, sharks in the Galápagos. Fear turned into respect.

V · The heights

Strangers turned into crew.

Annapurna at 4,130 m, passes across the Himalaya. The people on the trail taught me more than any classroom.

VI · The stillness

Handed, at the source.

Breathwork in Bali, Ayurveda in India — and in the Thar desert, the idea for all of this took shape.

What EducatedTraveler is: a skill, a place, a person, your people.

Every real skill is learned the same three ways — where it's alive, from a master who'll hand it over, beside the handful chasing the same thing.

“I never found my teachers. They found me.” Now I'm building the way in — the map I wish I'd had, and the doors it opens. The first one ran this October, in Martin Lippo's Barcelona laboratory.

Between collaborations I keep the Atlas — a record of where crafts are still taught at the source, ranked by community, not by who pays. Nobody buys a place on it: a craft opens when someone writes and asks for it.
