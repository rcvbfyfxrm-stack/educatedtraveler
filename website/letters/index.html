Letter Nº 2 Sent to the whole Circle · August 2026, before the week went public

Five days in Martin Lippo's lab

You plate a mousse in the galley. It's perfect. It goes up two decks in thirty degrees, and by the time it reaches the guest it's soup.

Gelatine melts at body temperature. Agar holds to about eighty-five. Nobody told me that at the time. I understood it much later, watching another chef reach for agar instead of gelatine, and he told me why.

If you cook privately — on a boat, in a house, for the same few people every day — you know that moment. The thing that kept going wrong, that no book ever quite fixed, until somebody standing next to you showed you in ten seconds.

I said I'd write when the first Lab Week was ready. It is, and it's five days of exactly that — in Martin Lippo's lab in Barcelona, 22–26 October.

It's built for private and yacht chefs. And it matters to me that the first is this craft. Cooking is what pulled me, about ten years ago, and it has carried me a long way since.

Why this, and not something easier. I've cooked fifteen years, most of them privately, and never went to culinary school. It started in Darwin, in the north of Australia, washing dishes in a Tex-Mex place. One afternoon after service the chef needed an extra hand. He gave me a knife and showed me how to cut. That's how I started.

Everything since, I learned standing next to someone who already knew. And when there was nobody, I read — everything I could find on the science of cooking, for years. But reading is one thing. Doing it with your own hands is what we're all craving, in reality. So I went.

And even then, I thought the modernist part was extra. Nobody needs a foam. I had it backwards. It was never about the foam. What you learn is what's happening when something goes wrong — the siphon that collapses, the emulsion that splits on day four, the sauce that has to hold six hours on a crossing. That's what lets you fix it at eight with guests already sitting, instead of starting again.

So I went and found the teacher. I'd followed Martin Lippo for years. Chefs I'd worked with had done his class, in Spanish. What existed was a class here and there, never the whole thing in English. So when I started EducatedTraveler he was the first person I emailed. We had never met and nobody put us in touch.

He answered, and we met at his lab in Barcelona. He got what I was trying to build faster than I expected, and started proposing things to do around the week himself.

Then he told me how he first came to Europe from Argentina. He didn't take a plane. He went down to the docks, found a sailing yacht looking for crew, and left on it — a famous racing boat, as it turned out. Three months aboard. Years later he went on a dive trip in Fiji as a guest, and still ended up in the galley, cooking a couple of the nights himself.

I grew up in the mountains of France, about as far from the sea as this country gets, and I got out the same way — by going where the boats were and asking. Strange thing to have in common with the man who's going to teach you spherification.

He's also the one who'll tell you straight that liquid nitrogen is rarely practical on a boat. I trust a teacher who tells you which of his own tools to leave at home.

Why Barcelona. These techniques were invented an hour and a half up the coast, at elBulli. When it closed in 2011 the chefs who built that language came back to Barcelona and opened restaurants here — Disfrutar, run by three of them, was named the best in the world in 2024. So here it's still a working trade, not history. It's also where my boat is, a 1979 schooner I'm slowly putting back together.

The week itself. Five days, thirty-five hours, hands in, in Martin's lab, 22–26 October, in English. Martin picked the techniques around what a galley actually needs. Texturisers, spherification, foams and siphons, sous-vide, nitrogen — which on a boat means prep that holds, ingredients that survive a long gap between ports, and service you can run alone.

You leave with a certificate signed at Vakuum for the thirty-five hours, the recipe and theory dossiers, and photographs of your own work shot by his team. Nobody honest promises you a job. But the next time a boat asks what you can actually do, you'll have something to show instead of something to say.

€1,500. It only runs if ten chefs have paid by 15 September — if that doesn't happen, everybody is refunded in full. I'd rather tell you that now than pretend. It goes public on Wednesday, so you're hearing it first, which is the point of the Circle.

The week itself is here. The craft, and where it's strongest in the world, is on the catalogue. And the long version of where all this came from is here.

I'm writing this from the galley in Yalıkavak, while the guests are off on a big day out.

If you're thinking about it, just hit reply: what does your October look like? That's the real question with this one. The money is the easy part; the calendar never is.

Hit reply, or message me on WhatsApp if that's easier. Both come straight to me, not to an office.

Until the next one,
— Arnaud
