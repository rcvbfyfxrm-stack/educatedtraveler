Pottery & Ceramics

In most Japanese crafts the lineage is a bloodline. In Mashiko it's a door — four generations of masters, and not one of them a son.

The town isn't ancient, the great man wouldn't sign his work, and the kilns you'll walk past are younger than they look. Here's what's true about it, what mingei actually meant, and where an English speaker gets their hands in the clay.

What it actually is

Mud, gravity, fire, and about a thousand hours

Throwing looks like magic for about four minutes, and then you try it. Centring — getting the lump to run true on the wheel before you ever open it — is the whole first month, and it's mostly a fight with your own shoulders. Beginners force it. Potters lean. What you're learning isn't a shape; it's how much pressure is exactly enough, and your hands learn that long before you can explain it.

Then the part nobody photographs. The pot dries, and it can crack. It gets trimmed, and it can collapse. It's glazed — chemistry you can't see until it's too late — and fired to somewhere past a thousand degrees, and the kiln has opinions. In a wood firing the flame itself marks the pot; where a piece sits in the chamber changes what comes out. You open the door a day later and find out what you made. Some of it is always broken. That isn't failure in this craft. It's the tuition.

Mashiko's own clay decides a lot of this. It's iron-rich, plastic and heat-resistant — easy to shape, forgiving in the fire, and dark enough that the town's signature glazes read against it: the persimmon-red kaki, the amber ame, the milky rice-husk-ash nuka. Pots from here tend to be sturdy and unfussy, and that's not an accident of taste. It's the ground.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about Mashiko

01

It's the young one. Japan's famous pottery towns — Bizen, Seto, Tokoname — are measured in centuries. Mashiko starts in 1853, when a potter named Ōtsuka Keizaburō, trained over in Kasama, found good clay at Ōtsusawa and built a kiln to make kitchen pots. By 1864 six workshops had the local domain's backing. In Japanese ceramic terms, Mashiko is barely out of its teens.

02

The most celebrated potter of the century refused to sign his pots. Hamada Shōji left his work unmarked — the mingei principle of the "unknown craftsman," the idea that a pot should be known by what it is rather than whose name is scratched on the foot. Those unsigned pots now sell at auction on the strength of the name he declined to write.

03

You don't have to be born into it. Most Japanese craft towns pass the trade down families. Mashiko took in outsiders — that's a large part of why it grew — and it still does. The two Living National Treasures the town produced were, in order, an art-school graduate from Tokyo and his student.

04

The climbing kilns you'll see are mostly younger than you are. On 11 March 2011 the Tōhoku earthquake put nearly every noborigama in town on the ground. Around 40% of the Hamada collection was destroyed. The potter Ken Matsuzaki lost every piece of his work and two of his three kilns. The tradition is a century old; a great deal of the brickwork was laid after 2011.

Where it comes from

Hamada, and the honest trouble with "the unknown craftsman"

In 1920 a young Japanese potter talked his way to England with the British potter Bernard Leach and spent three years with him at St Ives. He came home in 1923, and in 1924 settled in Mashiko — a working country pottery town, not a famous one — because the clay was good and the life was plain. His name was Hamada Shōji.

He became the central craftsman of mingei, the folk-craft movement built around the philosopher Yanagi Sōetsu, who coined the word and wrote its bible, The Unknown Craftsman. The argument was that the most beautiful objects in the world were made by ordinary people for ordinary use — without signature, without ego, in quantity. Hamada lived it about as far as a person can: he didn't sign, and he kept making the same domestic shapes for fifty years.

This work does not come out of my thought; rather I simply permit the movement that my hands have learned over many years. Hamada Shōji · quoted in Bernard Leach, Hamada, Potter (1975)

Here's the honest trouble, and any good teacher in Mashiko will tell you the same. The philosophy of anonymity produced one of the most recognisable names in ceramics. In 1955 Hamada was designated a Living National Treasure — the first person from the crafts ever named. When Yanagi died in 1961 he took over as director of the Japan Folk Crafts Museum. He was internationally famous, toured and filmed, and his unsigned pots are now identified, catalogued and priced by exactly the market the theory was written against.

That's not a scandal and it isn't hypocrisy. It's the tension the craft still lives inside: make useful things without vanity — in a world that will price your vanity anyway. Anyone selling you mingei as simple humble peasant-craft is selling you the poster. The real thing is more interesting, and less comfortable.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Mashiko ware begins 1853 — Ōtsuka Keizaburō, trained in Kasama (then Hitachi province, now Ibaraki), found high-iron clay at Ōtsusawa in the Kurobane domain and built a kiln for everyday kitchen goods; by 1864 six workshops had domain support.
  • Hamada Shōji (1894–1978) — travelled to England with Bernard Leach in 1920, three years at St Ives, returned to Japan in 1923, established his workshop in Mashiko in 1924.
  • Living National Treasure, 1955 — the first such designation ever made in the field of crafts. He succeeded Yanagi Sōetsu as director of the Japan Folk Crafts Museum on Yanagi's death in 1961.
  • "The unknown craftsman" — the phrase is Yanagi's, from the book of that title; Hamada's refusal to sign follows directly from it.
  • The quote above is Hamada's, recorded by Bernard Leach in Hamada, Potter (1975).

Where it's alive now

A chain of hands, and not one of them inherited

This is why we send people to Mashiko rather than to a prettier town with older kilns. The lineage here is documented, it's still working, and at every single link it was handed to a stranger.

1924 →

Hamada Shōji (1894–1978) arrives from Tokyo by way of St Ives. Not a Mashiko family. Living National Treasure, 1955.

1953 →

Shimaoka Tatsuzō (1919–2008), Hamada's leading disciple, works in Mashiko from 1953 until 2007 and becomes the town's second Living National Treasure. Hamada's student — not his son.

1972 →

Ken Matsuzaki, born in Tokyo in 1950, moves to Mashiko at twenty-two for a five-year apprenticeship under Shimaoka, then builds his own studio and kiln nearby.

2017 →

Florian Gadsby, a British potter born in Norfolk in 1992, spends six months as Matsuzaki's visiting apprentice — learning the Japanese kick-wheel, oribe and shino glazing, and how to fire them. He wrote the book about it.

Four links, a century, two continents, and the only qualification that recurs is that somebody showed up and was taken in. In Japanese craft that's rare enough to be the whole point.

What holds the town together now is unglamorous and real: more than 300 kilns, a working potters' community that absorbs newcomers from Korea, Taiwan, the United States and Europe, and two pottery fairs a year — Golden Week at the start of May, and the first week of November — when the whole place turns into stalls and the potters stand behind their own tables. The Hamada Shōji Memorial Mashiko Sankokan keeps his house, studio and climbing kilns as a working study collection rather than a shrine.

And it nearly ended. The 2011 earthquake took the kilns, the stock and, for some potters, everything they had ever made. They rebuilt. If you want to know whether a craft community is genuinely alive, that's the test — and Mashiko has already passed it in living memory.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the gift shop

Pottery is one of the most heavily sold "authentic experiences" on earth, and Japan is where the selling is slickest. The gap between the two versions is wide, and easy to see once you know the tells.

✓ The craft that lasts

Repetition on the wheel. Fifty of the same cup, thrown badly, then less badly. Every real potter's first year looks like this and there's no way around it.

Understanding the fire. Glaze chemistry, kiln packing, what flame does to a pot in a wood firing. This is the knowledge that separates a maker from a decorator.

Being in a working town. Eating with potters, seeing the seconds shelf, watching someone open a kiln and quietly bin a third of it.

✗ The costume that sells

The 90-minute "make your own bowl." You centre nothing; someone centres it for you, trims it later, glazes it later, fires it later, and posts it to you. Pleasant. Not the craft.

"Ancient tradition, unchanged for centuries." Mashiko is from 1853 and most of its kilns are post-2011. The truth is better than the brochure.

Wabi-sabi as a mood board. The imperfection you're being sold is usually a design choice made by someone in total control. Actual imperfection is the cracked half of the kiln load.

The con, where there is one, is time. A studio that quotes you an experience in hours is selling a souvenir; a studio that talks in days and weeks, and warns you you'll be centring for most of it, is teaching you. Ask how many of your own pots you'll actually finish, and who does the glazing and the firing. The answer tells you everything.

Where we'd send you

Two ways in, and they aren't the same door

Mashiko has an unusually honest shape to it: a genuinely open beginner's door, and a narrow one for people who already make. We'd rather show you both than pretend there's one path.

★ To get your hands in the clay

Mashiko Tougei Club — 益子陶芸倶楽部

Mashiko, Tochigi · Japan · English instruction available · live-in stays

Last checked · 17 August 2026The club is open (9:00–17:00, irregular closures), the English-teaching-staff line is still their own wording, and Furuki is still offered as lodging.

A working folk-style studio in the town itself, with the thing that matters most for a beginner who's travelled a long way: you can stay there. The club runs half-day and full-day classes with unlimited clay, has wood, gas and electric kilns, rents kiln space, and puts you up in Furuki, a restored kominka farmhouse with a sunken hearth. Their own words on language: "We have English teaching staff for both help with translation as well as instruction." That's rare in a Japanese craft town, and it's why they're the pick.

On languageEnglish instruction is offered, not assumed — Tochigi's tourism listings show an added fee for classes run in English. Confirm it when you book, and book the longest stay you can stand rather than a single afternoon.

What it opensHonestly? Not a job. What a serious stay gives you is a real beginning — centring, trimming, glazing, and a first firing you actually watched — plus people in a working pottery town who now know your face. That's the thing that lets you come back, and coming back is how apprenticeships in Mashiko have always started. Nobody there will promise you more than that, and neither will we.

How you'd start
  • Listed rates (via Tochigi tourism listings): roughly ¥4,800 for a half-day and ¥7,500 for a full day, 9:00–16:00, clay included; English carries an extra fee. Verify current prices direct — we quote a starting point, never a checkout.
  • Contact: mashiko-tougei-club.jp · 3288-6 Mashiko, Haga District, Tochigi · +81 285-72-3866. The site is mostly Japanese; the staff are not.
  • Time it with a fair — Golden Week (early May) or the first week of November — and you'll meet more potters in three days than in three months of any other week.
  • While you're there: the Hamada Shōji Memorial Mashiko Sankokan — his house, studio and climbing kilns.
★ If you already make

The Mashiko Museum Residency Programme

Mashiko Museum of Ceramic Art · ~2 months · open-call and invitation tracks

Last checked · 17 August 2026Link verified. The residency's open-call status was not re-confirmed at content depth.

The serious route. Running since May 2014, the residency brings ceramic artists from Japan and overseas to work in Mashiko for around two months, in a fully equipped studio with multiple kilns, wheels and mixers. The museum covers accommodation, airfare and studio fees; you cover your living costs. There's an open-call track and an invitation track for established artists.

The gate, shown straightTwo gates, in fact. It's for people with a body of work behind them, not beginners — and right now the open call is closed: the museum lists the next programme for spring 2028. We're telling you because a page that quietly routes you to a shut door is worse than no page at all. If this is your route, the useful move today is to write to residence@mashiko-museum.jp, get on their radar, and spend the intervening time making the work you'd apply with.

On languageThe programme runs internationally and publishes in English at mashiko-museum.jp/en/residence.

What it opensA residency at a named museum is a real line on a ceramics CV, and two months of studio access with local kilns is the kind of stretch that changes work. It's also, plainly, how you meet the Mashiko potters as a peer rather than a visitor.

And the hardest door, named honestly: a true uchi-deshi apprenticeship with a Mashiko master — the Matsuzaki route. There's no application form. It runs on introduction, usually from your own teacher, and it expects you to arrive already able to throw; Florian Gadsby came with three years under Lisa Hammond in London and an introduction made for him. Japanese helps enormously. If that's the life you want, the order is: learn to throw properly at home, find a teacher who knows someone, then go. Other real places, for context rather than as substitutes: Bizen, Seto and Tokoname — the older Six Ancient Kilns, mostly Japanese-language; Jingdezhen, China — porcelain at industrial depth, with an international studio scene; the Leach Pottery, St Ives (English) — the other end of the same century-old bridge, where Hamada learned.

Why you can trust this page

What we check before we send you anywhere

Everything on this page was verified against a source before it went up, and where we couldn't verify something, we left it out rather than made it pretty.

  • Is the community actually alive? Not a preserved village with a ticket office — potters who work, sell, argue and take in newcomers. Mashiko rebuilt its kilns after 2011; that answers the question.
  • Is the door genuinely open? We'd rather name a shut gate than route you into one. The residency's next open call is 2028, and it says so above.
  • Hours or weeks? If a place sells the craft in ninety minutes, it isn't teaching it.
  • Does the story hold up? Mashiko is from 1853, not antiquity, and the great man's anonymity is what made him famous. We'd rather tell you the awkward version.
  • Nobody pays to be here. No commission, no placement fee, no arrangement. If we wouldn't send a friend, it isn't on the map.
