Windsurfing & Wing-foil

The whole sport hangs on one free-swinging hinge between a sail and a board — which is exactly why, sixty years on, nobody quite agrees on who invented it.

Steer a rig by feel through your feet; then, when you're ready, trade it for a handheld wing and a foil that lifts you silently clear of the water. Here's what's real, what the boom sold, and where an English speaker actually learns it.

What it actually is

Two sports, one family, opposite hinges

Windsurfing looks like sailing standing up. It isn't. There's no rudder and no lines — the sail is joined to the board by a single universal joint, a free-moving hinge, and you steer the whole rig by tilting it: rake it back to bear away, forward to turn up into the wind, and read the rest through your feet. Fall, and you don't climb aboard a boat — you haul the sail out of the water by a rope and start again. It's less like helming and more like balancing a living thing that only stays up while you're honest with it.

Wing-foiling is the new cousin, and the beginner's mistake is thinking it's the same sport with a different sail. It's the opposite hinge. Here the wing is free in your hands — not rigged to the board at all — and the board rides a hydrofoil, an underwater blade that generates lift once you're moving, so the hull rises and you fly a foot above the water on almost no drag. One is a rig fixed to a board you tilt; the other is a wing you carry over a board that flies. Same wind, same balance, two very different feelings — and most people who love one end up doing both.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the board and sail

01

The entire sport is one hinge. The universal joint that lets the rig fall in any direction and be steered by tilt is the invention that made windsurfing possible — no joint, no sport. That's why the argument over "who invented the joint" is the argument over who invented windsurfing, and why it went to court.

02

A sailboard has hit 99 km/h. The windsurfing speed record is 53.49 knots — just over 99 km/h — set by France's Antoine Albeau on a 500-metre canal at the Lüderitz Speed Challenge in Namibia on 1 December 2024, ratified by the World Sailing Speed Record Council. On a board, with a sail, by feel.

03

It's been an Olympic sport since 1984 — and now it flies. Windsurfing debuted at the Los Angeles Games in 1984 on a one-design board with no harness allowed. Forty years later, the boards at Paris 2024 — the foiling iQFoil class — rise up on a hydrofoil and skim the water. The sport quietly turned into flight.

04

Wing-foiling barely existed before 2019. The first production wing shipped in the summer of 2019. It caught on because the foil kills the drag: you get flying in less wind than a windsurfer needs, and almost silently — no rig noise, no slap, just the hiss of the blade. A whole discipline, younger than most phones in your pocket.

Where it comes from

The tidy story is wrong, and the inventor said so first

The version you'll hear is that two Californians invented windsurfing: Jim Drake, an aeronautical engineer who'd worked on the X-15, and Hoyle Schweitzer, the businessman who turned it into the "Windsurfer." Their US patent — number 3,487,800, filed in 1968 and granted in 1970 — put the mast on a universal joint with a wishbone boom, and that design became the sport.

But it's not the whole truth. An American named Newman Darby had built and sailed a board with a handheld sail in 1964, and wrote it up in Popular Science in 1965 — he never patented it, because he couldn't afford to, and later watched others patent something close. The Smithsonian eventually took his prototype as the first American sailboard. And an English boy, Peter Chilvers, is credited with rigging a board back in 1958. The neat two-founder story is a marketing artefact; the real one is a tangle of at least three claimants and years of patent fights.

The most honest voice in the whole argument was Drake's own. Asked where he really stood among the inventors, the man who held the patent didn't reach for credit:

"I think I was probably no better than third." Jim Drake, on the other claimants Newman Darby and Peter Chilvers · American Windsurfer

That's the seed we'd rather plant than a hero myth: the person with the strongest legal claim pointing at the two people who came before him. If the inventor won't oversell it, neither will we.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • The patent: US 3,487,800, Drake & Schweitzer — filed March 1968, granted January 1970. Drake was the engineer; Schweitzer bought out his share and built the "Windsurfer" brand.
  • Newman Darby: built a handheld-sail board in 1964, published in Popular Science (1965); did not patent it (too costly). His prototype was later recognised by the Smithsonian as the first American sailboard.
  • Peter Chilvers: credited in England with a sailboard dating to 1958 — a claim upheld in later patent litigation as prior art.
  • Why it matters: the contested piece is the universal joint. Darby's rig was handheld and pivoting rather than the patented mechanical joint — which is exactly the distinction the courts and historians argue over.
  • Sources: americanwindsurfer.com — Origins of Windsurfing: Jim Drake · en.wikipedia.org — Jim Drake, Newman Darby, Windsurfing · invention.si.edu — Newman Darby, sailboard inventor.
Where it's alive now

From a Maui reef to a lake in the Alps

The sport's spiritual home is Maui, and the tell is that its two famous beaches sit at opposite ends of the skill ladder. Ho'okipa is the wave temple — expert-only, an unforgiving reef and a lineup of professionals, no place to learn. A few minutes away, Kanaha has flat water inside the reef and the summer schools; it's where the same island teaches beginners. One island, both ends of the sport.

Beyond Hawaii the scene clusters where the wind is reliable and named. Tarifa, on Spain's southern tip, is Europe's wind capital — the easterly Levante and westerly Poniente blow so dependably the whole town runs on them. Lake Garda, up in the Italian Alps, has the Ora: a warm afternoon thermal so predictable you can set a watch by it. And since 2019 the wing-foil boom has lit up all of them at once — flat, shallow bays that used to be quiet are now full of people learning to fly.

The honest read

The skill is real. The gear talk is the trap.

Wind sports have a particular con, and it isn't the sport — it's the shop. The learnable, durable thing and the thing being sold to you are easy to tell apart once you know the tell.

✓ The craft that lasts

Balance, wind-reading and rig control. The feel for where the wind is and how the board answers your feet — that's the skill, it's slow to build, and it carries across windsurf, wing and foil for the rest of your life.

Time on the water with a coach. Lessons in steady, safe conditions beat any board you could buy. Progress is hours in, not euros spent.

✗ The costume that sells

The next board, the lighter carbon, the new wing. The industry sells kit as if it's progress. Early on, better gear barely moves you — a good instructor and the right wind do.

"Fly on day one." Foiling is a genuine thrill, but the marketing that skips the fundamentals sells you a crash. The people who fly well are the ones who put in the unglamorous flat-water hours first.

So the tell is simple. If a place is selling you the newest rig and a fast-track to the foil, keep your wallet shut. If it's putting you in gentle wind with a coach and letting you fall a hundred times in safe water, that's the real thing — and it's the version that actually makes you good. Hours beat hardware.

On learning safely, plainly
  • Onshore vs offshore wind matters most. A good school teaches where the wind blows you back toward the beach, not out to sea. Ask which way it blows before you book.
  • Flat, shallow water is the beginner's friend — somewhere you can stand up and reset. Waves and chop come later.
  • Foiling has its own hazards — a hydrofoil is a blade. Reputable wing schools teach foil safety and impact protection from the first session; treat anyone who skips it as a warning.

Where we'd send you

Learn it in a bay that grows with you

★ Our pick for this craft

Vassiliki & the bay

Lefkada · Ionian Sea · Greece · May–Oct · English-language schools

Vassiliki is the bay Europe learned to windsurf in, and it's built for exactly the progression a beginner needs in a single day. Mornings bring a gentle onshore breeze over flat water — the safest, most forgiving place to make your first hundred mistakes. Then, most afternoons, a cross-shore thermal the locals nicknamed "Eric" fills in, and the same bay becomes a playground to grow into. Schools have run here for decades — Club Vass has operated in Vassiliki since 1987 — and the whole scene is dense, English-speaking, and used to taking people from zero. You can arrive unable to uphaul a sail and leave planing, with the wing world open in front of you.

On languageVassiliki's teaching scene grew up around British and international operators, so English is the default, not a translation you have to ask for. You're learning in your own language, in some of the friendliest teaching wind in Europe.

What it opensHere's the honest version, because you work near this world too: on a yacht, the water toys and tenders are the deck crew's job, and being genuinely at home on a board and a wing makes you the person who can run and teach them. But the credentials a boat actually asks for are the RYA powerboat and personal-watercraft tickets — windsurf and wing skill is what makes you useful with the toys, not a yacht qualification by itself. As a learner you walk away with a documented level (a VDWS or RYA card) that unlocks rentals and further courses worldwide. Not a promised job — a real, portable competence on the water.

How you'd start
  • Book a beginner course, not a board. A first windsurf course is a few mornings of theory, uphauling, and sailing across the wind in flat water — all coached, all in the safe part of the bay.
  • Mind the season. The thermal is a summer thing (roughly May–October) and afternoon-dependent — it doesn't blow hard every day, and the bay gets busy on strong "Eric" afternoons. Go in the season, and treat a light day as a gift for beginners.

Also real, if not the pick: Lac Bay, Bonaire (English) — the gentlest water of all: warm, waist-deep and flat for hundreds of metres, ideal if you want to add wing-foiling, though it's a longer, pricier trip. Tarifa, Spain (English schools) — Europe's wind capital and the busiest wing-foil scene, but the wind runs strong; better once you've got the basics. Maui — Kanaha (English) — the sport's home beach for learners, world-class but expensive, with Ho'okipa's expert reef next door as a reminder of where it can go.
