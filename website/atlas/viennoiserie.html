Viennoiserie

Everyone "knows" the croissant was born in Vienna the night the siege broke in 1683. It wasn't. The pastry you love is barely a hundred years old, French to the core, and built one cold fold of butter at a time.

Not an ancient secret and not really Viennese. A craft of dough, butter and temperature — here's what's real, what the legend sold, and where an English speaker actually learns to laminate.

What it actually is

A whole craft hidden inside one fold

Viennoiserie isn't a recipe, it's a technique — lamination. You make a plain yeasted dough, wrap it around a cold slab of butter, and roll the two out together into a long sheet. Then you fold it in three, turn it, and roll again. Each fold triples the layers: one becomes three, three becomes nine, nine becomes twenty-seven. Do it three times and you're carrying twenty-seven wafer-thin sheets of dough with butter between every one. That stack, and nothing else, is what a croissant is.

The magic happens in the oven. The butter trapped between the layers is mostly fat but partly water, and when it hits the heat that water flashes to steam and blows the layers apart before the dough sets around them. The honeycomb you see when you tear one open — that's the steam's work, frozen in place. Get the butter too warm and it soaks into the dough instead of staying in sheets; the layers weld shut and you've baked bread. The entire craft is keeping butter and dough at the exact same cool, workable temperature while you fold. That's why it's hard, and that's the thing a good school actually teaches.

The word covers a family, not one pastry: the croissant, the pain au chocolat, the pain aux raisins, the almond and the twice-baked. Brioche sits at the edge of it — enriched with butter and egg, but not always laminated. What ties the true viennoiseries together is the fold.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you over breakfast

01

The croissant is younger than the light bulb. The crescent roll is old, but the laminated croissant — the flaky one — doesn't show up in a French recipe book until the early 1900s. Auguste Colombié's encyclopedia around 1906 and Sylvain Claudius Goy's 1915 recipe are the first to fold butter through the dough. Before that, a "croissant" was a soft, brioche-like roll with no layers at all.

02

The man who brought it to Paris became a newspaper baron. August Zang, an Austrian ex-artillery officer, opened the Boulangerie Viennoise at 92 rue de Richelieu in 1839 and sold Vienna's crescent rolls from a patented steam oven. Parisians copied it, the word viennoiserie — "things in the Viennese style" — stuck, and Zang went home to Vienna to found Die Presse, a daily still publishing today.

03

Bakers use a different butter. Not the block from the fridge. Beurre de tourage — "roll-in butter" — runs around 84% fat against the usual 82%, which makes it drier and more pliable. It sheets out thin without cracking when it's cold or greasing into the dough when it warms. A degree or two of temperature is the difference between layers and a mess.

04

In France, the shape can tell you the fat. By long trade custom, a straight croissant is all butter (au beurre) and a curved, crescent one was often made with margarine. It's a convention, not a law — the artisan bakers' body says there's no rule on shape — but in a real French bakery it's a genuinely useful tell.

Where it comes from

Brought to Paris, and remade there

The story you've heard is that Vienna's bakers, working through the night during the 1683 Ottoman siege, shaped a crescent to mock the Turkish flag — and that Marie-Antoinette carried it to France. Both are folklore. The crescent roll, the kipferl, was a Viennese staple long before 1683, and no contemporary record ties the croissant to Marie-Antoinette. Historians have picked both stories apart for years.

What actually happened is smaller and truer. Zang's Boulangerie Viennoise put the Viennese kipferl in front of Parisians in 1839; French bakers loved it and imitated it. But the kipferl Zang sold was a dense, enriched roll — no layers. The leap came decades later, when French bakers married that crescent to their own pâte feuilletée, the laminated puff pastry France had been perfecting since the 1600s. Yeasted dough plus rolled-in butter — pâte levée feuilletée — and the modern croissant was born, in France, in the twentieth century.

The scholar who went through the Paris archives, Jim Chevallier, titled his book as plainly as the history reads — How Viennoiserie Came to France. Not born there. Brought there, then turned into something the Viennese never had. Jim Chevallier · August Zang and the French Croissant, 2009

So the honest seed of the craft is a borrowing that became a creation. The shape is Austrian. The layers — the whole reason a croissant shatters when you bite it — are French, and recent. Anyone selling you a thousand-year-old secret is selling the costume, not the craft.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • The siege myth: the kipferl predates the 1683 siege of Vienna; food historians treat the "invented to mock the Ottoman flag" tale as later folklore, not record.
  • Marie-Antoinette: no contemporary documentary evidence supports the story that she brought the croissant to France; it became popular there only in the 19th century.
  • August Zang (1807–1888): Austrian, son of a Vienna surgeon, former artillery officer; opened the Boulangerie Viennoise at 92 rue de Richelieu in 1838–39 with a patented steam oven; returned to Vienna in 1848 and founded the newspaper Die Presse.
  • The laminated recipe: the folded-butter croissant appears in print only in the early 1900s — Colombié (c.1906) and Sylvain Claudius Goy (1915) — built on French puff-pastry technique perfected from the 17th century.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Croissant · August Zang · Puff pastry · Kipferl; Institute of Culinary Education, "A Brief History of the Croissant"; Jim Chevallier, August Zang and the French Croissant.
Where it's alive now

Why the answer is still Paris

Viennoiserie has gone global — Tokyo and Melbourne and New York all bake extraordinary croissants now — but the place where it's a trade rather than a specialty is still France, and the densest, most competitive corner of that trade is Paris. Here boulanger is a regulated profession with a state diploma behind it, not a hobby word. The city runs an annual Grand Prix de la Baguette; bakers chase the Meilleur Ouvrier de France collar; and every arrondissement has a handful of bakeries laminating by hand at five in the morning, arguing about butter percentages the way other cities argue about coffee.

That density is the point. In Paris you can taste ten serious croissants in a morning's walk and start to feel the difference between a good lamination and a lazy one — the shatter, the open honeycomb, the clean butter finish. You learn the standard by eating your way to it, then you learn the hands from people who hit it every day. This is where the craft's "your people" actually are.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

Few crafts are romanced as hard as this one, and the romance points exactly away from the part that's difficult to learn.

✓ The craft that lasts

Lamination and temperature. Folding, resting, and keeping butter and dough at one cool temperature the whole way. This is the skill — unglamorous, physical, and the only thing that separates a croissant from a bread roll.

Reading the dough. Knowing when the butter is about to break, when to rest, when to push. It transfers to every laminated thing — pain au chocolat, kouign-amann, Danish, puff.

✗ The costume that sells

The "ancient Viennese secret." The siege legend, the mocked-flag crescent, the royal courier. Charming, and false. It's sold because history feels like flavour — but it teaches you nothing about the fold.

The one-morning "authentic" class. A tourist croissant workshop that hands you a certificate and a photo. Real lamination lives across days of resting and folding; an hour with pre-made dough is a nice memory, not a skill.

So the tell is simple. If a place is selling you a legend and a laminated photo op, it's selling the costume. If it's making you chill the butter, fold, rest, and fold again until your hands learn the temperature — that's the craft, and it's more satisfying than the legend ever was. The boring version is the real one.

On the myths and the shapes, plainly
  • 1683 siege: no contemporary evidence; the crescent roll predates it. A good story retrofitted onto an older pastry.
  • Straight vs curved: by trade custom the straight croissant is pure butter and the curved one often margarine — but France's artisan bakers' body (CNBPF) confirms there is no official rule on shape. Useful convention, not law.
  • Puff pastry ≠ croissant: a croissant is yeast-leavened laminated dough (pâte levée feuilletée); classic puff pastry is not yeasted. Same fold, different rise.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Croissant · Puff pastry; Tasting Table and CNBPF on croissant shape conventions; Institute of Culinary Education.

Paris — FERRANDI's viennoiserie fundamentals

Paris · France · English intro courses · CAP available in French

FERRANDI Paris is one of France's leading professional culinary and pastry schools, and it runs short "Introduction to the Fundamentals" courses in bread baking and viennoiseries — taught in English, built for beginners and career-changers. You spend the time doing the one thing that matters: laminating dough with your own hands, in the city where the standard was set. It's the honest entry point — real technique, a serious school, no legend for sale.

On languageFERRANDI's fundamentals intros run in English. If you want the deeper professional route, Le Cordon Bleu Paris teaches its Boulangerie diploma and its "Freshly Baked Pastries" workshop in French with consecutive English translation — croissants, pain au chocolat and brioche among them. You don't have to trade the source for a language you speak.

What it opensLamination is a portable, hireable skill — it's the same fold behind croissants, Danish, kouign-amann and puff, and every bakery and pastry kitchen needs someone who can do it cold. It also carries straight into a boat galley or a café that wants real breakfast. Not a promised job — a technique that makes you genuinely useful anywhere bread and butter meet.

How you'd start — and the credential, honestly
  • The real French credential is the CAP Boulanger — a state diploma (Certificat d'Aptitude Professionnelle), and the qualification you legally need to set up as a baker in France. It's taught in French and takes months to two years. A weekend school certificate is not that; a serious school will tell you which one you're getting.
  • Start short and hands-on. A multi-day fundamentals course in viennoiserie teaches the fold, the rest, and the temperature — enough to know whether you want the long road. Confirm you're laminating your own dough, not decorating pre-made croissants.
  • Then eat. Before and after any class, taste widely across Paris bakeries. The palate you build is half the training.

Also real, if not the pick: Le Cordon Bleu Paris (French with consecutive English translation) — the fuller diploma route. École Internationale de Boulangerie, Provence (French, English support) — immersive bread-and-viennoiserie training away from the city. École Nationale Supérieure de Pâtisserie and other French schools run professional viennoiserie modules — excellent, mostly in French.
