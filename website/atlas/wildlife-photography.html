About this skill

Wildlife Photography

Among the first to make a craft of wildlife photography were two farmers’ sons from Thwaite, in Yorkshire’s Swaledale. In 1892 Cherry Kearton made the first photograph ever taken of a bird’s nest with eggs, and with his brother Richard he learned that the camera had to disappear: they worked from inside a hollow ox in 1900 and a stuffed sheep in 1901. The hide is still the heart of the craft, and its modern form was born at the edge of Pusztaszer, in Hungary, by the wetlands. There, in the spring of 2005, Bence Máté began photographing from behind one-way glass, so shy animals are not disturbed. He is the only photographer to have won the Wildlife Photographer of the Year grand prize as both a young and an adult entrant.

Gold credential: No universal credential — a published portfolio and a Wildlife Photographer of the Year placing are the real currency. BA (Hons) Marine & Natural History Photography (Falmouth) is the nearest formal route. · No universal body — portfolio and prize based. SINWP offers voluntary nature/wildlife qualifications, PPA a general Certified Professional Photographer credential, and NANPA is the main association. Wildlife Photographer of the Year (Natural History Museum, London) is the recognised summit.

What you learn at the source
  • Hide craft. Working unseen from a hide; behind one-way glass you can be within metres of an undisturbed bird.
  • Long-lens technique. Shooting with a long lens from the hide or the vehicle, still and ready for the moment.
  • Reading light. Working dawn and dusk, and waiting until the light, the animal and the frame arrive together.
  • Patience, then the edit. Sitting out a subject, then the middle of the day for editing and critique.

Learn to work a one-way glass hide

in Pusztaszer, Hungary
Season: Apr-Aug · Beginner -> Professional

A national park directorate settles this, not a hide: Kiskunság publishes 22,225 hectares of protected landscape, five thousand of them internationally recognised wetland, and over 280 bird species recorded. The birds are there because the ground is protected. ⚠ A hide holds one to five people, so the company is small by design — and the man who invented the technique here does not tutor these groups.

One-way glass hide photography was pioneered here by Bence Máté — the only person to hold both Young Wildlife Photographer of the Year and Wildlife Photographer of the Year — from his family's house by the wetlands at the edge of Pusztaszer.

Learn to photograph big cats from a camp

in Maasai Mara Conservancies, Kenya
Season: Jun-Mar (set departures Jun, Dec, Jan, Mar) · Beginner -> Professional

The conservancies' own association publishes the shape of it: 24 member conservancies over about 450,000 acres, leased from more than fifteen thousand Maasai landowners. That is ground held by the people who live on it rather than a park with hides in it. One operator here, and the man whose camps they are guides the departures himself.

The most photographed big-cat ground on earth, worked from conservancies rather than the crowded reserve. Paul Goldstein has run his own camps here for decades and guides the departures himself, which is rarer than it sounds.

Learn to sit a bear hide on the Russian border

in Kuhmo, Finland
Season: Apr-Aug (evenings and nights); Sep-Mar (daytime) · Beginner -> Professional

One operator, run by the man who effectively invented this kind of tourism — nearly four decades of taking other people to the same bog, which is a lineage held by one person rather than a scene. ⚠ One to five in a hide is the whole of the company, and nothing published by anyone else describes the place.

Lassi Rautiainen has been photographing these bears since the 1970s and taking others to them since 1991, and around twenty individual brown bears still work the bog in front of his hides — along with wolves and wolverines.

Catalogued, not checked

Fourteen more places we know of, and nobody has checked any of them

Listing five places would tell you this craft happens in five places, and it does not. These are the rest we know of. Nobody has checked any of them. No school here is vetted, no teacher named, nothing graded — this is the lowest state on the map, and we would rather print it than let a short list read as a complete one. A line each, and what the place is known for. If you have been to one, or know a school or an instructor there, the line will take a note — it comes to me, and it is how a place stops being a line and starts being checked.

  • Svalbard, Norway
    High Arctic, worked from a ship — polar bear, walrus and light that lasts all night in summer.
  • Churchill, Manitoba, Canada
    Polar bears on the tundra in a short autumn season, from vehicles built for it.
  • Bandhavgarh & Ranthambore, India
    Tiger photography under a permit-and-zone system that decides what you can shoot.
  • Pantanal, Brazil
    The best jaguar photography in the world, from boats, in a defined dry season.
  • Galápagos, Ecuador
    Animals with no fear of people, and rules that keep you a fixed distance from them anyway.
  • Torres del Paine, Chile
    Puma tracking on foot with local trackers, in hard weather.
  • Bwindi & Volcanoes, Uganda / Rwanda
    Gorilla photography in dark forest, on a permit, with an hour to work.
  • Yellowstone & Grand Teton, United States
    Wolves, bears and rut behaviour, all reachable by road in winter.
  • Katmai, Alaska, United States
    Brown bears fishing at close range in a July and September season.
  • Antarctic Peninsula, Antarctica
    Penguins and whales from a ship, on a short summer window and at real cost.
  • Hortobágy & the Danube Delta, Hungary / Romania
    Central European wetlands with hide networks of their own, close to Pusztaszer's tradition.
  • Varanger & Båtsfjord, Norway
    Arctic seabirds and winter ducks from floating hides, in cold that decides the day.
  • Extremadura, Spain
    Raptors, storks and dehesa woodland, with hides run by local landowners.
  • Bharatpur (Keoladeo), India
    A man-made wetland that became one of Asia's great bird sites, worked on foot or by rickshaw.

Why you can trust this map

How a place gets on here, and what we do when it stops being true

There is a real difference between a school that teaches you and a good-looking website, and too much of the internet is the second kind. Four instruments do the work, and every one of them is visible on the page you are already on.

  • We grade it in public, and publish what is missing. The Measure is five plain questions, not a score: is there a teacher we can name, can a stranger get in, will you be learning beside other people, is the craft alive in the place, and is there enough there to keep you going for years. An empty dot tells you which one we could not answer and why. A craft nobody has graded shows no meter at all, because an absence is not a zero.
  • We print what we checked and did NOT list. Every swept craft carries the places we looked at and turned down, each with its reason. That list is the part no marketplace prints, because a rejection is where the money is.
  • The claims are read back to their sources every night. A robot re-reads every link we publish against the school's own page each night, and every price and course name we have written a test for. page. When one stops matching three nights running, it is flagged for a human — and an entry that cannot be confirmed comes off. It is never softened.
  • Where we are not neutral, we say so above the recommendation. We run one paid week a year, with one teacher. His entry says that before it says anything good about him, and prints the way to reach him without us, at his prices.
  • Nothing here has been stood in yet. An open sheet means desk research, carefully done. No place on this map has been visited and dated by us. When one is, the check will carry a name and a date — and until then, the grade says so in the dots it leaves empty.
  • Nobody pays to be here, and nobody can pay to be left off. No commission, no listing fee, no tourist board. The order is the strength of the community, never the size of the wallet.
Before you trust any school — mine or anyone else's — ask these five things
  1. Who actually teaches it? Can you find them by name, with a track record you can check yourself?
  2. Is the craft alive in that place, or is the school the only thing there? A real scene has more than one good option.
  3. What exactly do you walk away with — a recognised qualification, or a certificate they printed themselves? Ask which.
  4. Can you speak to someone who did the course? A real person, not a testimonial on their own page.
  5. What happens on a bad day — weather, an injury, a teacher who doesn't show? A serious place has an honest answer.

If a place dodges these, that's your answer. It costs you nothing to ask, and it tells you everything.
