About this skill

Safari & Wildlife Guiding

Guiding people on foot through wild country, to watch animals rather than shoot them, has one of its first grounds in South Africa. By 1958 half of South Africa’s iMfolozi Game Reserve was proclaimed a wilderness area, among the first official wilderness areas in Africa. In 1959 Ian Player and the game tracker Magqubu Ntombela led the first official Natal Parks Board trail into it, and the trails they began are still walked today. Today the craft is most alive in South Africa, where the Field Guides Association of Southern Africa, founded in 1990 and based in Hoedspruit, sets the standards guides train and are assessed against. In 2024 it helped found a sister association for East Africa, built on those same standards.

Gold credential: FGASA Professional Field Guide (NQF2) + Apprentice Trails Guide · FGASA (Field Guides Association of Southern Africa) / CATHSSETA; KPSGA in East Africa

What you learn at the source
  • Tracking on foot. Reading spoor and sign on the ground, then following an animal's trail on foot.
  • Identification. Naming the mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and trees around you, and the behaviour you are watching.
  • Ecology, geology and weather. How the bush works as a whole: its ecology, the rock beneath it and the weather over it.
  • Leading drives and walks. Planning and leading game drives and walks, with navigation and radio procedure; in the Okavango, by mokoro too.

Strong living community

in Maasai Mara / Laikipia, Kenya
Season: Jul-Oct (migration) · Beginner -> Professional Guide (KPSGA)

The conservancies' own association publishes what holds this ground: 24 member conservancies, more than fifteen thousand landowners, about 450,000 acres, with lease fees paid to those landowners every year. Guiding here is the landowners' own livelihood rather than a course sold on their land. One school runs a camp inside it.

Where it began
Birthplace of the discipline

Learn to guide on foot in the wilderness where South Africa’s walking trails began, and qualify on the same ground.

in Zululand (Hluhluwe & iMfolozi), South Africa
Season: May-Sep (dry season); intakes January and August · Beginner -> Professional Field Guide

One school has its base camp here, and FGASA's own list of delivery partners is where we found it rather than its own site. What is older than the school is the trail: the Wilderness Leadership School has been walking people into this wilderness since the late 1950s, and the two men who started it are named below. Nobody teaching here today is named on any page we can find, which is the gap to ask about before you book.

South Africa’s walking trails began on this ground: by 1958 half of iMfolozi had been proclaimed a wilderness area, among the first in Africa, enterable only on foot, on horseback or by canoe, and guides have been taking people into it on foot ever since.

Catalogued, not checked

Ten more places we know of, and nobody has checked any of them

Listing five places would tell you this craft happens in five places, and it does not. These are the rest we know of. Nobody has checked any of them. No school here is vetted, no teacher named, nothing graded — this is the lowest state on the map, and we would rather print it than let a short list read as a complete one. A line each, and what the place is known for. If you have been to one, or know a school or an instructor there, the line will take a note — it comes to me, and it is how a place stops being a line and starts being checked.

  • Mana Pools & Hwange, Zimbabwe
    Some of the most demanding walking-safari country in Africa, with a famously rigorous national guide exam.
  • South Luangwa, Zambia
    The valley where the walking safari was invented, and where guides still qualify to lead on foot.
  • Serengeti & Ngorongoro, Tanzania
    The migration ecosystem — enormous, heavily visited, and the standard against which others are described.
  • Nyerere (Selous), Tanzania
    Vast, wild and lightly trafficked, with boat and walking access rather than road.
  • Etosha & Damaraland, Namibia
    Desert-adapted wildlife and a landscape that teaches tracking because cover is thin.
  • Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, South Africa / Botswana
    Red dunes and predator viewing in a park run jointly across a border.
  • Madikwe & the Waterberg, South Africa
    Malaria-free reserves close to Johannesburg where many South African guides do their first seasons.
  • Queen Elizabeth & Kibale, Uganda
    Savannah and primate work in one country, with a separate guiding qualification for gorilla and chimp tracking.
  • Ranthambore, Kanha & Bandhavgarh, India
    Tiger country, with a state-licensed naturalist system that works nothing like FGASA.
  • Pantanal, Brazil
    The densest large-mammal viewing in South America, and a guiding tradition built around boats and horses.

Why you can trust this map

How a place gets on here, and what we do when it stops being true

There is a real difference between a school that teaches you and a good-looking website, and too much of the internet is the second kind. Four instruments do the work, and every one of them is visible on the page you are already on.

  • We grade it in public, and publish what is missing. The Measure is five plain questions, not a score: is there a teacher we can name, can a stranger get in, will you be learning beside other people, is the craft alive in the place, and is there enough there to keep you going for years. An empty dot tells you which one we could not answer and why. A craft nobody has graded shows no meter at all, because an absence is not a zero.
  • We print what we checked and did NOT list. Every swept craft carries the places we looked at and turned down, each with its reason. That list is the part no marketplace prints, because a rejection is where the money is.
  • The claims are read back to their sources every night. A robot re-reads every link we publish against the school's own page each night, and every price and course name we have written a test for. page. When one stops matching three nights running, it is flagged for a human — and an entry that cannot be confirmed comes off. It is never softened.
  • Where we are not neutral, we say so above the recommendation. We run one paid week a year, with one teacher. His entry says that before it says anything good about him, and prints the way to reach him without us, at his prices.
  • Nothing here has been stood in yet. An open sheet means desk research, carefully done. No place on this map has been visited and dated by us. When one is, the check will carry a name and a date — and until then, the grade says so in the dots it leaves empty.
  • Nobody pays to be here, and nobody can pay to be left off. No commission, no listing fee, no tourist board. The order is the strength of the community, never the size of the wallet.
Before you trust any school — mine or anyone else's — ask these five things
  1. Who actually teaches it? Can you find them by name, with a track record you can check yourself?
  2. Is the craft alive in that place, or is the school the only thing there? A real scene has more than one good option.
  3. What exactly do you walk away with — a recognised qualification, or a certificate they printed themselves? Ask which.
  4. Can you speak to someone who did the course? A real person, not a testimonial on their own page.
  5. What happens on a bad day — weather, an injury, a teacher who doesn't show? A serious place has an honest answer.

If a place dodges these, that's your answer. It costs you nothing to ask, and it tells you everything.
