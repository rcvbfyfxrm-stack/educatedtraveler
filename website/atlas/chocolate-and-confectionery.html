Chocolate & Confectionery

Chocolate isn't candy — it's a fermented fruit. And the flavour the whole industry chases was bred, centuries ago, into a fragile Venezuelan bean that most "fine" chocolate never touches.

A near-extinct white bean, a walled village you reach by boat, and a chef who won gastronomy's biggest prize for teaching women to make chocolate from the seed. Here's what's real, what's sold, and where you'd actually learn it.

What it actually is

Made on a drying patio, not in a factory

Chocolate starts as the seeds of a tropical fruit, the cacao pod — bitter, purple, wrapped in white pulp. What turns them into flavour isn't the sugar or the shine; it's fermentation. The beans are piled with their pulp and left to ferment for days, and that week is where most of the aroma is made — or lost — before anyone roasts, grinds or tempers a thing. "Confectionery" is the finishing craft that sits on top: tempering, the bonbon, the filled praline, the smooth snap. Real skill, worth learning. But the honest headline is that the bean and its ferment set the ceiling, and a great chocolatier reveals a good bean rather than rescues a bad one.

So there are really two crafts wearing one name, and reading past the marketing means holding both. There's making — fermenting, roasting and grinding cacao into chocolate, "bean-to-bar," which lives best where the cacao grows. And there's finishing — the European bonbon-and-praline tradition, perfected in Switzerland, Belgium and France, which mostly buys its chocolate in. Learn where each one actually happens and the whole business gets easier to see.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the bean

01

The best cacao is almost white. The prized "noble" variety, criollo, includes Porcelana — a pale, ivory bean from around Venezuela's Lake Maracaibo, so low in bitterness it's been called the Rolls-Royce of cacao. The colour is the tell: almost no purple pigment, almost none of the harshness. It's vanishingly rare.

02

Fine cacao is a rounding error. The hardy workhorse bean, forastero, is roughly 80% of the world's cacao. True fine-flavour criollo is well under 5% of production — by some counts nearer 1%. Almost everything sold as chocolate is made from the workhorse, not the noble bean.

03

One of the world's great cacaos comes from a village with no road. Chuao, on Venezuela's Caribbean coast, sits pinned between mountains and sea and is reached mainly by boat. Its beans are dried on the plaza in front of the village church, a ritual barely changed in centuries — and Chuao carries one of the first denominations of origin ever granted to a cacao.

04

A country once ran on it. In the first half of the 1700s Venezuela was the world's leading cacao supplier. The plantation elite of colonial Caracas were literally called the "Grandes Cacaos" — and in Venezuelan Spanish the phrase still means a big shot. The country's identity and its cacao grew up together.

Where it comes from

The chef who put the school back at the source

The chocolate story we're told is European: Swiss conching, Belgian pralines, French bonbons. That's the finishing school, and it's real — but it imported the flavour. The source is the forest where the bean grows, and the person who has done the most to put learning back there is a Venezuelan chef, María Fernanda Di Giacobbe. In Caracas she built a lab and school, Cacao de Origen, on one plain idea: teach people — most of them women — to make chocolate from the seed, in the country where the seed is best. In 2016 that idea won her the first-ever Basque Culinary World Prize, the €100,000 award the press calls the "Nobel of gastronomy," chosen by a jury that included Ferran Adrià and Massimo Bottura from more than a hundred candidates.

I come from a country that is a matriarchy — where the family economy is always run by mothers, grandmothers and aunts, where all our cooking and all our cacao has been preserved by women. María Fernanda Di Giacobbe · translated from Spanish

What she was honoured for wasn't a bar or a shop. It was a network — teaching, small enterprise and research built around cacao as Venezuelan identity, moving people from home cook toward maker in the communities where the bean actually grows. That's the honest correction to the myth: chocolate's flavour was never a European invention perfected in a factory. Europe perfected the machine and the bonbon. The bean, and the knowledge of it, belong where it's grown — which is exactly the argument her school is built to make.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Basque Culinary World Prize: Di Giacobbe won the inaugural edition in 2016; a €100,000 prize, selected by an international jury of chefs from 100+ candidates across some 30 countries. The press widely nicknames it the "Nobel of gastronomy" — a nickname, not an official title.
  • Cacao de Origen: her Caracas lab and school, teaching the full chain from bean to bar and training chocolate-makers — heavily women — as micro-entrepreneurs. She also holds Venezuela's Tenedor de Oro (Golden Fork), 2015.

Where it's alive now

The bean-to-bar movement, and its spiritual home

The most interesting thing happening in chocolate isn't in a European factory — it's the bean-to-bar movement: small makers fermenting, roasting and grinding cacao at origin, so the farmer, the fermenter and the maker stand close together instead of a continent apart. It's a scene you can join — origin makers across Ecuador, Peru and Colombia, a growing craft-chocolate community that talks openly about farms, varieties and ferments the way winemakers talk about vineyards.

Its spiritual home is Venezuela — Chuao, the Sur del Lago, Ocumare, and Caracas where Di Giacobbe teaches. That's where the noble bean was bred and where the deepest knowledge sits. It's also, right now, one of the hardest places on earth to reach responsibly — which is a real part of this craft's honest story, and where the next section has to be straight with you.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the sticker

Few foods are sold with more mythology than chocolate. The gap between what makes it good and what's printed on the wrapper is wide — and easy to read once you know where to look.

✓ The craft that lasts

Bean and ferment first. Fine chocolate is decided on the farm and the drying patio — variety, fermentation, careful roasting. Skill reveals a good bean; nothing rescues a badly-fermented one.

Bean-to-bar at origin. Making chocolate where the cacao grows, close to the farmer — the honest version of "single-origin," where the word actually means something.

✗ The version that sells

The percentage number. A high "%" tells you how much of the bar is cacao, not how good it is — it can just mean more of an over-roasted, poorly-fermented bean.

"Raw" chocolate and health halos. Fermentation routinely heats beans past the "raw" threshold, so most "raw" chocolate isn't — and rawness was never what made cacao good.

So the tell is simple. If a bar is selling you a number, a health claim or the word "single-origin" with nothing behind it, you're buying the sticker. If the maker can tell you the farm, the variety and how the beans were fermented, that's the real thing — and it tastes like it. The version that talks about the bean is the good version.

On percentages, "raw," and the three-variety story
  • Cacao % is a ratio, not a grade. It's the share of the bar from cacao (mass + butter), and says nothing about bean quality, fermentation or roast. A well-made 70% beats a careless 85% every time.
  • "Raw" is mostly a myth. Good cacao is fermented, and ferment temperatures commonly climb above the ~46–48°C "raw" threshold — so most chocolate labelled raw isn't, and it was never the point.
  • Criollo / forastero / trinitario is a useful shorthand that modern genetics has complicated; "pure criollo" claims deserve a little skepticism. Origin and variety matter — but only alongside good post-harvest work.
  • Sources: perfectdailygrind.com — raw cacao; the myth of the 3 cacao varieties · barandcocoa.com · vice.com (raw chocolate).

The source, honestly — and where you can actually go

★ The source of this craft

Caracas & Cacao de Origen

Venezuela · the home of criollo · a working producer school

This is where the noble bean was bred and where the deepest knowledge lives, and Di Giacobbe's school is the truest expression of "learn it from the seed" anywhere in chocolate. Two honest things, though. First, Cacao de Origen is a working school for local producers — most of them women — taught in Spanish; it isn't a tourist workshop with a booking button, and we won't pretend it is. Second, and bigger: Venezuela in 2026 is under severe travel advisories. The UK advises against all travel; US guidance has swung between "Reconsider Travel" and "Do Not Travel," and the US has had no full embassy in Caracas since 2019, so there's little consular safety net. That makes Caracas a source you honour, not a trip we can responsibly sell you right now.

On language & accessSpanish, and built for Venezuelan producers rather than visitors. If and when it's genuinely safe and open to travellers, it's the first place we'd want to send you. Until then, the responsible path is to learn the same craft — bean-to-bar, at origin — somewhere you can actually reach.

What it opensFermenting, roasting and finishing cacao is a real trade: it points toward work as a chocolatier or bean-to-bar maker, toward cacao sourcing and quality, or toward your own small-batch craft — the "home cook to maker" path Di Giacobbe's own school is built on. Not a promised job — a craft that makes you genuinely useful in any kitchen or workshop that takes chocolate seriously.

How you'd start
  • Start with a hands-on bean-to-bar day at origin — roast, grind, temper, and taste your way through varieties. It's the cheapest honest test of whether the making grabs you as much as the eating.
  • If it holds, look at a multi-day origin program (farm + ferment + bar) or a structured course in confectionery/bonbon craft. Learn the making first; the finishing tradition sits neatly on top of it.
  • On Venezuela: if you're set on the source, check travel.state.gov and your own government's advice the week you plan anything — the situation moves, and this page won't send you somewhere unsafe.

Where you can actually go now (English-forward, real and bookable): Ecuador (English) — multi-day programs where you work a cacao farm, ferment, and make bars in the Amazon and coastal cacao country. Peru — ChocoMuseo, Cusco (English) — hands-on bean-to-bar workshops, easy to book. Colombia — Cartagena (English) — bean-to-bar and cacao workshops at the edge of fine-cacao country. And for the finishing craft — the bonbon, the praline, tempering — the European schools of France, Belgium and Switzerland (English widely available) are the real source, labelled for what they are: finishing, not growing.
