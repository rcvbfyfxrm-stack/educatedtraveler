Sake & Sommellerie of Sake

Everyone calls it rice wine. It's neither wine nor beer — it's the one drink on earth made by a mould and a yeast working the same tank at the same moment.

A grain that gets milled down to its heart, a fungus that turns starch to sugar, a yeast that eats the sugar as fast as it appears. Here's what actually happens in the tank, what's craft and what's marketing, and where an English speaker learns to read it at the source.

What it actually is

Not wine. Not quite beer. Its own thing.

Wine is easy: crush grapes, the sugar is already there, yeast turns it to alcohol. Beer needs one extra step — malting, to free the sugar locked in grain — and then it ferments. Sake does something no other drink does: it frees the sugar and ferments it at the same time, in the same tank. A mould called kōji — Aspergillus oryzae — grows through steamed rice and breaks its starch into sugar, while sake yeast eats that sugar into alcohol the instant it appears. Brewers call it multiple parallel fermentation, and it is the whole secret.

That trick is why sake is so strong. Because the sugar never piles up — it's consumed as fast as the mould makes it — the yeast keeps going far past where grape or grain would stall, reaching 18 to 20% alcohol before it dies, the highest of any brewed drink on earth. Most sake is then watered back to around 15%. The rice itself is a different plant from what's on your plate: sake rice (sakamai) is milled hard before brewing to strip away the fat and protein in the outer grain and reach the clean, starchy heart. How far it's milled — the seimaibuai, or polishing ratio — is the number you see on every good bottle.

And "sommellerie of sake" is a real craft, not a borrowed word. It's learning to read all of that in the glass: to taste the difference between a bottle where distilled alcohol was added (honjōzō) and one made of nothing but rice, water, kōji and yeast (junmai); to serve it at the temperature that flatters it — some of the best sake is cold, some is warm, and warming to hide a fault is where the "always hot" myth came from; and to sit it beside food. The Japanese don't even call the drink "sake" — in Japan that word means any alcohol. This drink is nihonshu.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the cup

01

A fungus does the work, and it's the national one. Aspergillus oryzae — kōji — is officially recognised as Japan's national fungus, designated by the country's Brewing Society. The same mould is behind miso, soy sauce and mirin. When UNESCO added Japanese sake-making to its heritage list, the thing it was really honouring was a thousand-year relationship with a mould.

02

UNESCO agreed, and only just. On 4 December 2024, "traditional knowledge and skills of sake-making with kōji mould in Japan" was inscribed on UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — Japan's 23rd entry, and only the second for its food culture after washoku itself. It is a young honour for a very old craft.

03

Some bottles throw away most of the rice. The most famous premium sake, Dassai 23, is milled until only 23% of each grain is left — 77% ground away, roughly seven days of continuous polishing per batch, to reach the pure starch at the centre. Daiginjo grade means at least half the grain is gone. It is deliberate, expensive subtraction.

04

The strongest fermentation in the world happens quietly. No spirit is distilled, no alcohol forced — yet parallel fermentation pushes sake to around 20% before the yeast simply dies of its own alcohol. Nothing else brewed gets close. The power comes from patience and a mould, not a still.

Where it comes from

Chewed rice, then monks, then a stone that settles it

The oldest rice alcohol in Japan wasn't brewed at all — it was chewed. In kuchikami-zake ("mouth-chewed sake"), people cooked rice and chewed it so the enzymes in their saliva would turn the starch to sugar; the mash was spat into a tub and left to ferment. The romantic telling — shrine maidens, the saliva of virgins — is the part that gets repeated. It's real, and it has almost nothing to do with the drink you'd order today. What changed everything was kōji: a mould that did the saliva's job, cleanly and at scale, adopted from around the Nara period.

The clear, filtered sake we now drink was perfected not in a distillery but in a Buddhist temple. At Shōryaku-ji, in the hills outside Nara, monks worked out the techniques that still define the craft: a stable, contamination-resistant yeast starter (bodaimoto), the three-stage build of the fermentation, and morohaku — using polished white rice for both the kōji and the mash. That is the backbone of every bottle since.

"The birthplace of Japanese refined sake." The stone monument raised at Shōryaku-ji, Nara, 1999

The temple isn't a museum piece about it, either. Every January, the sake brewers of Nara still gather at Shōryaku-ji to make the bodaimoto starter together, by hand, the old way — a living thread running straight back to the source. Which is the honest shape of sake's origin: not one inventor, but a mould, a temple, and a craft passed hand to hand for a thousand years.

Where it's alive now

Two rival waters, and a trade thinning out

Sake's centre of gravity has long sat in the Kansai, split between two towns that argue through their water. Nada, on the coast at Kobe, brews with hard, mineral-rich miyamizu water that ferments fast and firm — its sake is dry and sturdy, nicknamed otoko-zake, "men's sake." Fushimi, in the south of Kyoto, uses soft water that ferments slow and gentle, giving a rounder, more fragrant style called onna-zake, "women's sake." Same rice, same mould — the water writes the character. Nada alone, the district of Nada-Gogo ("the five villages"), is the largest sake-producing area in Japan, with around thirty breweries in a walkable stretch, several with English-language museums and tasting rooms.

But the living scene is also a thinning one, and an honest guide says so. Japan had over 3,000 breweries in 1970; today it's closer to 1,200. The old system of migrant master brewers — the tōji, who led seasonal guilds down from farming villages each winter — is fading fast; many guilds are defunct, and more owners now brew themselves. That decline is exactly why the craft is worth going to meet while the people who carry it are still standing in the room.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the number

Sake has one big number bolted to it — the polishing ratio — and a whole marketing language grew up around making it mean "better." It helps to separate what's true from what's sold.

✓ The craft that lasts

The kōji, the starter, the temperature. Growing the mould evenly through the rice, building a clean yeast starter, and holding a cold fermentation for weeks — that is the skill, and it's what a good tongue can taste.

Reading and serving it. Knowing junmai from honjōzō, matching a bottle to a temperature and a dish, telling a fault from a style — the sommellerie is a real, learnable craft, and it travels.

✗ The costume that sells

"More polished = better." A lower seimaibuai costs more and tastes cleaner, not automatically finer. Milled too hard, sake loses the savour that made it interesting — many brewers now argue less polishing makes better sake.

"Real sake is served hot," "sake is rice wine." Both are tourist shorthand. Warming was often used to mask cheap sake; and calling it wine hides the one thing that makes it singular — the mould.

So the tell is simple. If a place sells you a number on the label and a hot cup no matter the bottle, you're being sold a costume. If it talks about the kōji, the water, the yeast and the temperature it should be poured at — that's the real thing, and it's the part you can actually learn to taste. The quiet version is the good version.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Multiple parallel fermentation: kōji (Aspergillus oryzae) saccharifies rice starch to sugar while sake yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments the sugar to alcohol, simultaneously in one tank — the reason sake reaches ~18–20% ABV, the highest of any brewed drink, before yeast dies off past ~20%.
  • Grades by polishing: daiginjo/junmai daiginjo ≤50% of the grain remaining; ginjo/junmai ginjo ≤60%; honjōzō ≤70%. Junmai's old 70% floor was removed in 2004 — junmai now simply means no distilled alcohol added, only rice, water, kōji and yeast.
  • The national fungus & UNESCO: Aspergillus oryzae is designated Japan's national fungus by the Brewing Society of Japan; "traditional knowledge and skills of sake-making with kōji mould in Japan" was inscribed by UNESCO on 4 December 2024.
  • Terminology: in Japan "sake" means any alcohol; the drink is nihonshu. Seishu ("clear sake") is the legal category (National Tax Agency): under 22% ABV, fermented from rice, kome-kōji and water, and strained.

Where we'd send you

Learn to read it in the country that makes it

★ Our pick for this craft

WSET Level 3 Award in Sake — in English, in Tokyo

Tokyo · Japan · in English · WSET-approved provider

The sommellerie of sake is taught, examined and graded — and the credential the trade actually reads is the WSET Level 3 Award in Sake. It was built by sake specialist Natsuki Kikuya with Master of Wine Antony Moss and technically edited by Japan's National Research Institute of Brewing, so it isn't a tourist tasting — it's the serious one. And you can sit it in English, in Tokyo, at a WSET-approved school such as the Caplan Wine Academy: a real exam in the country the drink comes from, with the breweries an afternoon's train ride away. Do the classroom, then spend your other days at the source — that's the trip we'd send you on.

On languageLevel 3 Sake is delivered in English in Tokyo, so there's no trade-off between the best source and a language you speak. Instruction, tasting and the exam are all in English; the sake in the glass is as Japanese as it gets.

What it opensA Level 3 pass is a recognised sake qualification that restaurants, importers and sake bars around the world know by name — a genuine foot in the door for anyone in wine, hospitality or the drinks trade, and a real test of whether reading sake for a living is for you. Not a promised job — a credential the industry reads, earned where the drink is made.

How you'd start — and what the certificate is really worth
  • It's a real, fail-able exam. Level 3 asks for about 42 hours of study and tests both written theory and blind tasting. It is a qualification, not a certificate of attendance — you can fail it, which is what makes passing it mean something.
  • Pair the paper with the place. Base yourself for a week: the classroom in Tokyo, then Nada-Gogo in Kobe — the largest sake district in Japan, thirty-odd breweries with English museums and tasting rooms — and, if you can time it, the birthplace at Shōryaku-ji, Nara.
  • Start smaller if you're new. WSET runs Level 1 and Level 2 in Sake as well, also in English; they're the gentle on-ramp if Level 3's blind tasting sounds like a lot on day one.

Also real, if not the pick: International Kikisake-shi (English) — the Japan-native qualification from the Sake Service Institute, a sommelier-level title that leans into sake within Japanese cuisine and service; a strong alternative if you want the domestic credential rather than the WSET one. WSET Level 3 Sake outside Japan (English) — the identical qualification is taught by approved providers in London, the US and Hong Kong if you can't get to Tokyo; same exam, less atmosphere. Fushimi, Kyoto (mostly Japanese) — the softer-water counterpart to Nada, with the Gekkeikan brewing museum, if you want the other half of the rivalry.
