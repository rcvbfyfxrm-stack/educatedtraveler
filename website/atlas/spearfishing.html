Spearfishing

Everyone pictures the shot. The shot is the last, easiest part — spearfishing is a held breath and a stalk, and the most honest way there is to catch a fish: you choose the exact one, or you go home empty.

No tank, no net, no bycatch. Just lungs, patience, and one clean take — the oldest hunt on Earth. Here's what's real, what gets sold, and where an English speaker actually learns it without getting hurt.

What it actually is

The gun is the least of it

Beginners fixate on the speargun. Every good teacher spends the first days elsewhere — on the breath. Spearfishing is freediving with a purpose: you drop on a single lungful, hang still, let a fish forget you're there, and take one shot from close. The physiology is the same as freediving's, which is why serious spearos get their freediving certificate first and why the sport's first law is the freediver's law — one down, one watching the surface, every time. Miss the breath and none of the rest matters.

It splits into a few worlds worth knowing before you book. Reef and shore hunting is the way in — shallow water, a short swim out, fish you can name. Bluewater hunting is the open ocean, miles offshore, drifting a float and waiting on pelagics — tuna, wahoo, dorado — that can outweigh you; it's advanced, and it's where the sport gets mythologised. And competition, run by CMAS and national federations, scores a bag of fish inside strict rules — no scuba, ever. By law and by rule, this is done on breath alone.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the hunt

01

Half your snorkel kit was invented for spearing fish. The modern swim fin was patented by a French naval officer, Louis de Corlieu, on 2 June 1933. A year later Georges Beuchat opened his Marseille workshop; his shop gave the sport the rubber-band "Tarzan" speargun in 1947 and the surface float in 1948. The whole recreational snorkelling world is a by-product of Mediterranean hunters.

02

It almost went to the Olympics. Competitive spearfishing organised fast: the first contest ran in the United States in 1947, and the first world championship was held at Mali Lošinj, then Yugoslavia, in 1957. CMAS calls it the most selective fishing method there is — you see and choose the single fish, so there is no bycatch.

03

The founding book was written by a fighter pilot. Guy Gilpatric — an American aviator turned novelist living on the French Riviera — wrote The Compleat Goggler in 1938, the first how-to guide to the sport. He taught a young Jacques-Yves Cousteau and Hans Hass to put their faces underwater. The gear came later; the goggles and the held breath came first.

04

The danger isn't the fish — it's the breath. Shallow-water blackout takes the fit, calm, experienced diver near the surface, with no air-hunger and no warning. That single fact is why "I'll just take one quick dive alone" is the sentence that drowns strong swimmers, and why every real school teaches one-down-one-up before it teaches you to aim.

Where it comes from

A novelist, a pair of goggles, and the Riviera

The tidy story credits Cousteau. The honest one starts earlier and drier. In the late 1920s and 1930s, on the Mediterranean coast of France and Italy, a handful of swimmers began sealing watertight goggles over their eyes and diving down to spear fish — years before the aqualung existed. The man who wrote it all down was Guy Gilpatric, a WWI fighter pilot who'd turned to writing and settled on the Côte d'Azur.

His 1938 book The Compleat Goggler — the title a wink at Izaak Walton's The Compleat Angler — was the first guide to the sport: how to make the goggles and spears, how to stalk, how to cook the catch. Cousteau read it and credited it for pushing him underwater. So the sport didn't grow out of scuba. Scuba grew up alongside a hunt that was already happening on one breath.

The first and only exhaustive treatise on the art of goggle fishing — that most noble and excellent sport, perfected and popularised in the Mediterranean Sea, though long practised elsewhere by other benighted savages. Guy Gilpatric · The Compleat Goggler, 1938 (the book's own subtitle)

Then the gear-makers turned a pastime into a sport. Corlieu gave it fins; Beuchat's Marseille shop gave it the banded speargun and the float. The point of telling it straight: the romance of spearfishing is real, but it was never a lone hero and a big gun. It was patient people in cold Mediterranean water, learning to hold still.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • The founding text: Guy Gilpatric (1896–1950), The Compleat Goggler, first published 1938 — widely described as the first comprehensive guide to goggle-diving and spearfishing; he influenced Cousteau and Hans Hass on the Riviera.
  • Swim fins: Louis de Corlieu, a French naval officer, filed his fin patent in Paris on 2 June 1933 ("propulseurs de natation et de sauvetage").
  • The speargun & float: Georges Beuchat founded Beuchat in Marseille in 1934; the elastic-band "Tarzan" speargun dates to 1947 and the surface buoy to 1948.
  • Competition: first spearfishing contest in the USA, 1947; first CMAS-era world championship at Mali Lošinj (then Yugoslavia), 1957.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Spearfishing · Guy Gilpatric · Louis de Corlieu · Georges Beuchat · Beuchat · cmas.org · sportalsub.net (CMAS championship archive).
Where it's alive now

From the Med to a handful of blue coasts

The birthplace never let go: the French, Italian, Spanish and Croatian Mediterranean still runs the deepest competition culture, the federations, and the record books. But a hunt needs clear water and fish, and the community grew wherever both are — the Sea of Cortez in Mexico and Kona in Hawaii for bluewater and reef; South Africa, Australia, Florida and San Diego for their own strong scenes.

What separates a real place from a pretty one isn't the fish — it's the culture around the breath. The scenes worth travelling to are the ones where people freedive first and hunt second, where a licence is normal, and where you'll find a buddy who'll watch your surface for the next ten years. That's the thing you're actually looking for: not a spot, a crew.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

Spearfishing sells two versions of itself, and they look nothing alike — one is a skill you build for years, the other is a photo.

✓ The craft that lasts

The breath and the stalk. Calm freediving, reading current and light, getting close enough that the shot is easy. Selectivity is the whole ethic: you take the one fish you chose, and nothing else dies.

Freedive first, always. A recognised freediving foundation and one-down-one-up discipline. It isn't red tape — it's the difference between a sport and a drowning.

✗ The costume that sells

The big gun and the hero shot. The giant-fish photo, the trophy bag, depth and size chased for the feed. It's the loudest part of the sport and the smallest part of the skill.

"Grab a gun and go." A charter that hands a beginner a loaded speargun and motors them to deep water with no freediving training and no buddy plan. That's not access — that's how fit swimmers die.

So the tell is simple. If a place leads with the gun, the trophy and the deep drop, walk. If it leads with the breath, the buddy and the local law — and makes you a freediver before it makes you a hunter — that's the real thing. The patient version is the one you get old doing.

On safety and the law, plainly
  • Shallow-water blackout is a loss of consciousness from low oxygen near the surface at the end of a breath-hold — often with no warning. It overwhelmingly takes fit, experienced divers. A buddy at the surface is the only thing that turns a blackout into a scare instead of a death.
  • Never dive alone, and never hyperventilate to "hold longer" — it blunts the urge to breathe without adding oxygen. Good schools teach you to stop.
  • Scuba + speargun is banned in competition and in most jurisdictions; the sport is breath-hold by definition.
  • Licences vary wildly by country. In France, for example, the only legal must is third-party liability insurance, available through a federation for around €20 a year; elsewhere it's permits, size limits, no-take zones and protected species. Check the local rule before you get in — that's part of the craft.

Where we'd send you

Learn it warm, clear, and in English

★ Our pick for this craft

Kona & the Big Island

Hawaii · United States · year-round · freedive-first schools

Kona is one of the sport's true classrooms. A steep drop-off sits minutes from shore, the water is warm and clear all year, and the reef gives you a gentle way in while the blue water is right there when you're ready. Best of all for a beginner: the scene teaches freediving first — you can walk in unable to hold your breath and leave a calm, certified freediver who happens to now hunt, with the safety drilled in before the gun.

On languageHawaii is native English — instruction, safety briefings and the licence rules all in plain English. You don't trade the best water for a language barrier here; they're the same place.

What it opensSpearfishing done right leaves you with a real freediving foundation — the same skill that carries into scuba, dive-guiding, underwater work and simply being useful and calm in the sea. It's a genuinely sustainable way to put food on a table, and for cooks and yacht crew it's a skill that travels. Not a promised job — a competence that makes you more at home on the water, and worth more on it.

How you'd start
  • Get freedive-certified first (AIDA, Molchanovs, PADI or SSI Freediver) — typically 2–3 days of theory, safety and buddying. This is the gate that keeps you alive; do it before you touch a speargun.
  • Then a guided reef session with an instructor who runs strict one-down-one-up, teaches you to read the water, and shows you the shot from close range — not a boat that just hands you a gun.
  • Learn the local law — Hawaii has size limits, protected species and gear rules. Knowing them is part of hunting honestly.

Also real, if not the pick: Côte d'Azur — Cassis to Antibes, France (mainly French) — the birthplace, deep competition culture, and the only legal must is ~€20/yr federation insurance; the source, if you don't mind the language. Sea of Cortez & Cabo, Mexico (Spanish, English-friendly) — world-class bluewater for tuna and wahoo, better suited once you're past the basics.

Why you can trust this page

What we check before we send you anywhere

Spearfishing gets sold as a trophy shot, and the gap between a real school and a good-looking website is wider here than almost anywhere. Here's the bar a place has to clear before it goes on the map.

  • Freedive first is the culture, not the upsell — if a place will put a beginner in deep water with a gun and no freediving foundation, it's off the map.
  • One-down, one-up, every dive — buddy discipline taught from minute one. Looseness here is disqualifying.
  • Straight about the law — licences, limits, protected species. A serious place tells you the local rules; a careless one leaves you to find out the hard way.
  • Nobody pays to be here. If we wouldn't put a friend in that water with that teacher, it isn't on the map.

It isn't about the biggest fish. It's about being quiet enough to get close — and coming up, every time.
