Cheese & Fermentation

Cheese is the oldest way we ever cheated time. Not milk kept fresh — milk rotted on purpose, and the whole craft is learning to steer the rot instead of fear it.

Salt, a curd, a cave, and a few microbes doing the real work. Here's what's true, what the label quietly hides, and where you'd actually learn to make it.

What it actually is

Applied rot, kept on a leash

Left alone, milk spoils. Cheese is what happens when someone decides how it spoils instead of letting chance decide. Bacteria eat the milk sugar and turn it to acid; an enzyme called rennet sets the acidifying milk into a solid curd; you cut it, drain the watery whey, salt it, press it, and then hand it to time. Nothing is being preserved from decay — the decay is the process, aimed and slowed until it tastes like something you'd pay for.

There are only four levers, and every cheese on earth is a different setting of them: the milk (which animal, which pasture, raw or pasteurised), the culture (which microbes you invite in), the rennet (how you set the curd), and the affinage (how long, how cool, how damp you age it). Change one and you can walk from a fresh, squeaky curd to a hard wheel aged three years. That's the whole instrument.

And cheese is only the most patient member of a much bigger family. The same move — invite the right microbes, starve the wrong ones, control salt and time — is what makes bread rise, wine turn, miso deepen, and cabbage keep as sauerkraut for a winter. Learn to read it in cheese and you've learned to read fermentation everywhere.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about cheese

01

It's older than writing. Perforated clay strainers dug up in Kuyavia, Poland still carry the chemical trace of dairy fat — the first hard evidence of cheese-making, from roughly 7,000 years ago. People were straining curds from whey more than a thousand years before anyone in Mesopotamia pressed the first written word into clay.

02

Most hard cheese was never vegetarian — and the fix was one of the first GM foods. Traditional rennet is an enzyme from a calf's stomach. In 1990 the US approved fermentation-produced chymosin — the calf gene inserted into a microbe so it brews the enzyme in a tank — the first genetically engineered ingredient in the food supply. It now sets an estimated 90% of hard cheese, and it's why a cheese can be labelled vegetarian at all.

03

Roquefort's blue is a mould, farmed by wind. The veins are colonies of Penicillium roqueforti, ripened in the natural Combalou caves at Roquefort-sur-Soulzon, where cracks in the rock called fleurines pull cold, damp air through the cellars on their own. In 1925 it became the first cheese France ever granted an Appellation d'Origine — a place, protected by law.

04

One cheese is fermented by live maggots. Sardinia's casu marzu is left open for cheese flies to lay in; the hatching larvae digest the fat and turn the paste soft and weeping. Guinness World Records once called it the world's most dangerous cheese, and selling it commercially is illegal in Italy — which has not stopped the shepherds who still make it.

Where it comes from

No inventor — but fermentation got its proof in one small valley

You'll hear the legend everywhere: a desert nomad carried milk in a pouch made from a young animal's stomach, the enzymes and the heat did their work on the ride, and by evening he had curds. It's a good story. It is also unprovable folklore — cheese has no inventor and no birthday, only 7,000 years of people, in a hundred places, arriving at the same trick.

What does have a name is the moment we understood what was happening. For most of history, souring and rising and turning were thought to be spontaneous — chemistry that just occurred. Then a chemist proved it was alive. In 1857 Louis Pasteur — born in Dole and raised in Arbois, in the Jura — showed that milk turns to acid because living microbes are eating it, not because of some property of the milk itself. He ran much of that work from a modest lab and a vineyard in his home valley, and used it to demolish the idea that microbes appear from nothing.

La fermentation, c'est la vie sans air. — fermentation is life without oxygen. Louis Pasteur · Études sur la Bière, 1876

Here's the honest part we don't skip: the same Pasteur also gave us pasteurisation. Heating milk to kill microbes made dairy safe and shippable — and, a century on, it's also what let industry strip out the wild microbial life that gave traditional cheese its depth. The man who proved fermentation is alive handed us the tool most used to quiet it. Both things are true, and a good cheesemaker lives in the tension between them.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Oldest cheese: milk-fat residue in perforated Linear-Pottery-culture sieves from Kuyavia, Poland, ~5200–4900 BCE — the first unequivocal evidence of cheese-making in Europe (Salque et al., Nature, 2012). Milk residues elsewhere are older; processed cheese here is the earliest proven.
  • Pasteur: born Dole, 1822; raised in Arbois, Jura. His 1857 memoir on lactic-acid fermentation attributed souring to living microorganisms; his Arbois-based studies (Études sur le Vin, 1860–64) and vineyard experiments disproved spontaneous generation.
  • The quote: "La fermentation, c'est la vie sans air" is Pasteur's aphorism from Études sur la Bière (1876) — his physiological theory of fermentation as life without free oxygen.
  • Pasteurisation: patented by Pasteur in the 1860s to preserve wine and later milk — genuinely lifesaving, and the hinge on which the raw-milk-vs-industrial cheese argument still turns.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Louis Pasteur · Cheese · History of cheese; nature.com — Salque et al. 2012; montagnes-du-jura.fr — Pasteur in Arbois.
Where it's alive now

Two scenes: a French mountain, and a fermentation lab

The craft is loudest in two very different places. The old world is the Jura and Franche-Comté, in eastern France — the same valleys Pasteur came from. This is Comté country: France's single largest protected-cheese sector, roughly 70,000 tonnes a year made in village co-operative dairies called fruitières, then handed to a handful of aging houses. One of them, Marcel Petite, ripens Comté inside Fort Saint-Antoine, a decommissioned 19th-century military fort in the mountains — around a hundred thousand wheels resting in stone, a place the trade calls the cathedral of Comté.

The new world is the fermentation renaissance that ran out of Copenhagen. When Noma built a dedicated fermentation lab and its team published what they were doing, they turned koji, miso, lacto-ferments and garums from restaurant secrets into something home cooks and young chefs everywhere now practise. Cheese sits inside that wider revival — the most demanding, slowest room in a house that fermentation built.

Why the Jura wins for actually learning it: nowhere else stacks the whole craft this densely — the national dairy school, the working co-operatives, the aging cellars, and the history, all within a short drive, in a landscape that has done nothing else for centuries. It's where the craft is a livelihood, not a demonstration.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

Cheese carries more marketing romance per gram than almost anything on the Atlas, because two very different products wear the same warm words.

✓ The craft that lasts

Real milk, read and steered. Raw or gently handled milk from a known herd, live cultures, a curd set and cut by feel, and months of genuine affinage in a cool, damp room. The flavour comes from a living microbial community — the thing you're actually learning to manage.

Place that means something. An Appellation or protected name ties a cheese to a region, a breed and a method. It isn't a marketing badge — it's a rulebook you can go and check.

✗ The costume that sells

"Artisan" and "farmhouse" on industrial curd. Neither word is regulated. Plenty of cheese in a rustic wrapper is standardised, pasteurised, factory-set with tank-brewed chymosin, and aged by machine — fine to eat, just not the picture on the label.

"Aged" with no cave behind it. Vacuum-sealed months in a warehouse is not affinage. The romance of tradition is sold hardest by the producers who kept the least of it.

The con isn't poison — most industrial cheese is perfectly good food. The con is the story: paying a craft price for a factory product because the label whispered "traditional." The tell is simple and it costs nothing to ask. What milk, whose farm, how long did it age, and where? A real cheesemaker answers in seconds and enjoys the question. A marketing department changes the subject.

On the microbes, plainly
  • Fermentation-produced chymosin (FPC) — the calf-rennet enzyme brewed by genetically modified microbes — was approved in the US in 1990, the first GM ingredient in the food supply, and now sets an estimated 90% of hard cheese. It's considered molecularly identical to calf chymosin and safe; it's simply not "a calf and a cave."
  • Raw vs pasteurised is the live argument of the craft. Raw milk carries the wild microbial community many great cheeses depend on; pasteurisation trades some of that depth for safety and shelf life. Serious makers on both sides can defend their choice — the point is that it is a choice, made for reasons.
  • "Artisan," "farmhouse," "cave-aged" are largely unregulated words in most markets. Protected names (AOP/PDO, and the like) are the ones with an enforceable definition behind them.
  • Bronwen and Francis Percival's Reinventing the Wheel is the clearest book-length case that a cheese is, above all, a microbial ecosystem — and that industrial hygiene quietly flattened it.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Chymosin · Rennet · Roquefort · Comté cheese; fda.gov — chymosin GRAS 1990.
Where we'd send you

Learn it where it's a livelihood

★ Our pick for this craft

Poligny & the Jura

Franche-Comté · France · ENILEA (national dairy school) · Comté country

Poligny calls itself the capital of Comté, and it earns it: the town holds ENILEA, France's national dairy school, sitting in the middle of the fruitières and aging cellars it trains people for. The school transforms six to seven thousand litres of milk a day into cheese as teaching, and puts around seven hundred trainees a year through the trade. You are not watching a workshop here — you are standing in the working heart of the craft, with the co-operatives, the caves and Pasteur's own valley within reach.

The gate, honestlyENILEA's real cheesemaking training — the fromager tracks, from the CAP through professional and continuing-education routes — is taught in French, and the serious ones are multi-month, often apprenticeship-based commitments, not a drop-in week. If you want to make cheese with your hands soon and in English, start elsewhere and come here when you're ready to go deep.

The English pathThe School of Artisan Food on the Welbeck Estate in Nottinghamshire, England, teaches cheesemaking in English — from one-day introductions to a multi-week Foundations of Professional Cheesemaking, run by working dairy professionals. It's the cleanest English-language on-ramp to the same craft.

What it opensA genuine foundation in making and aging cheese: the ability to set a curd you understand, to read a ripening room, and — for those who go the distance — to run a micro-dairy or take affinage work. ENILEA's qualifications are recognised across the French dairy industry. Not a promised job — a craft, and a set of doors that only open once you can actually do it.

How you'd start
  • Try it before you commit. A one- or two-day cheesemaking course (the School of Artisan Food, or a good local dairy) tells you fast whether you love the patience of it or not.
  • Then choose your depth. The English short-course ladder builds real skill; the French ENILEA route builds a recognised professional qualification. Different tools for different plans — be honest with yourself about which you want.
  • Ask the four questions of any school before you pay: what milk, whose method, how much hands-on time, and what exactly you walk out holding.

Also real, if not the pick: Roquefort-sur-Soulzon & the Combalou caves (French) — to see affinage as geology, in the caves that earned France's first AOC. Copenhagen & the wider fermentation scene (English-friendly) — not a cheese school, but the home of the modern ferment revival cheese belongs to. Vermont and the US farmstead scene (English) — a young, serious raw-milk cheese culture, if you'd rather learn closer to home.
