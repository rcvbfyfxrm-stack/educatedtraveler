Surfing

The world thinks California invented it in the sixties. Hawaiians had been riding waves for centuries — until it nearly died out, and one Waikiki swimmer carried it back to the world.

Not a lifestyle you buy at a surf camp. An ancient craft of reading the ocean, most of it spent paddling and waiting. Here's what's real, what the camps oversell, and where a beginner actually starts.

What it actually is

Mostly you are just sitting in the sea

The image is a person carving a blue wall of water. The reality, especially at the start, is that surfing is a lot of paddling, a lot of waiting, and a lot of reading the ocean to be in exactly the right place at exactly the right second. Hawaiians called it he'e nalu — "wave sliding" — and treated it as something between a sport and a sacrament. The riding is the smallest part. The craft is everything that gets you there: paddling strength, wave selection, the timing of the pop-up, and the etiquette that keeps a crowded lineup from turning into chaos.

That's the honest shape of the learning curve, too. A first lesson gets you standing in the whitewater on a big soft-top board — a real and joyful thing, and nowhere near "surfing" in the sense the videos sell. Reading a real wave, paddling into it unassisted, and turning along its open face is a skill measured in seasons, not sessions. The ocean is the teacher, and it does not grade on a curve.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things the surf-brand version leaves out

01

It was written down in 1779 — and it was already ancient. Lieutenant James King, finishing Captain Cook's voyage journals after Cook was killed in Hawai'i, watched islanders ride the surf and called it "most perilous and extraordinary." That's the first European account of surfing, nearly two centuries before California's beaches learned the word.

02

Your board announced your rank. In old Hawai'i surfing was stratified by class. Chiefs — the ali'i — rode long olo boards of up to fourteen or sixteen feet; commoners rode shorter alaia boards, and certain breaks went with certain ranks. It wasn't a pastime. It was woven into the order of society.

03

It very nearly disappeared. Through the 1800s, missionary disapproval combined with a catastrophic collapse of the Hawaiian population from introduced disease and the unraveling of traditional life. Surfing was never formally outlawed by statute — but by the end of the century it had all but vanished, surviving in pockets. What we have now is a revival.

04

It took a century to reach the Olympics — as its greatest champion had asked. Surfing finally debuted at Tokyo 2020 (held in 2021); the first gold medals went to Ítalo Ferreira of Brazil and Carissa Moore of Hawai'i. Duke Kahanamoku had called for surfing's inclusion after winning swimming gold back in 1912.

Where it comes from

Duke Kahanamoku carried it back from the edge

The man who saved surfing wasn't a surfer by trade — he was a swimmer. Duke Kahanamoku, born in Honolulu, won Olympic swimming gold at Stockholm in 1912 and again at Antwerp in 1920 — five Olympic medals, three of them gold. He was also a Waikiki beachboy, and everywhere his swimming took him he brought a surfboard. In December 1914 he paddled out at Freshwater Beach near Sydney and gave a demonstration Australians still commemorate; in California he did the same. More than anyone, Duke carried a nearly-lost Hawaiian practice back out into the world.

The myth to retire is that surfing is a twentieth-century California invention — sun, woodies, a pop soundtrack. That era popularised and commercialised surfing; it did not create it. And even Duke, rightly called the father of modern surfing, is better understood as its greatest ambassador than its inventor: the practice was centuries old, and the revival had other hands in it too. The honest version is older, deeper, and Hawaiian.

One of the most perilous and extraordinary things I ever beheld … and well deserving a distinct relation. Lt. James King · Cook's voyage journals, 1779 — the first written account of surfing

That line, written by an outsider watching for the first time, is the honest starting point of the whole story: surfing was already old, already astonishing, long before anyone thought to sell it.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Duke's medals: gold in the 100 m freestyle at Stockholm 1912 and Antwerp 1920, relay gold in 1920, plus relay silver (1912) and 100 m silver (1924) — five Olympic medals, three gold.
  • Australia: his Freshwater Beach, Sydney demonstration in late December 1914 (with a follow-up in January 1915) is marked with a plaque and statue there today.
  • Ambassador, not inventor: surfing predates Duke by centuries; he is the great populariser of the modern era, alongside figures like George Freeth and the wider Hawaiian community.
  • The 1779 quote: from the official published journals of Cook's third voyage, completed by Lt. James King after Cook's death at Kealakekua Bay — the earliest European description of he'e nalu.
  • Sources: olympics.com & ishof.org — Duke Kahanamoku; environment.nsw.gov.au — Freshwater blue plaque; eos.surf & Museum of British Surfing — the 1779 James King account.
Where it's alive now

Waikiki is where the modern story starts and hasn't stopped

Surfing is everywhere now — a world tour, a global industry, coastlines on every continent. But its modern heart is still Waikiki, the gentle stretch of Honolulu where Duke grew up, where the beachboy tradition of teaching visitors to surf began, and where a nine-foot bronze statue of Duke stands on Kūhiō Beach with his arms open and fresh lei around his neck. The beachboys are still there, still putting first-timers onto waves.

And there's a reason beginners belong here specifically: Waikiki's summer south swells roll in slow, soft and long — one of the world's classic learner waves. It's warm all year, the water is forgiving, and you're learning in the exact place the modern sport was reborn. You go to Waikiki for the wave and for the lineage at once — the people on that beach have been teaching this for a hundred years.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the brochure

Few crafts have been marketed as hard as surfing, so the gap between the practice and the pitch is worth naming plainly.

✓ The craft that lasts

Ocean literacy. Reading swell, wind and current; paddling; patience; the etiquette of a lineup. This is the slow apprenticeship that actually makes a surfer, and it carries to any coast on earth.

A living heritage. He'e nalu is a real Hawaiian tradition with a real lineage — learning it at Waikiki connects you to something centuries deep, not to a brand.

✗ The brochure that sells

"Surf in a week." A soft-top whitewater ride on day one is genuine fun — but sold as if it were surfing a real wave, it sets a false clock. Standing up isn't the same as surfing.

The lifestyle package. The barrel photo, the spring-break surf camp, the idea that the gear and the look are the point. The look is not the craft.

So the tell is honesty about time. A good school is upfront that your first lesson is a beginning, teaches you to respect the ocean and the locals, and never pretends a week makes you a surfer. If they sell you the barrel on day one, keep looking.

On the water, plainly
  • A first lesson typically gets you standing in whitewater on a big, soft, stable board — real progress, and a modest, honest one.
  • The ocean has rules. Currents, crowded lineups, reef and rocks in places, and a right-of-way etiquette that keeps people safe. A serious school teaches these before technique.
  • Reading and riding an open, unbroken wave is measured in seasons of regular practice — not in a single trip. Anyone promising otherwise is selling the brochure.

Where we'd send you

Learn it warm, gentle, and where it was reborn

★ Our pick for this craft

Waikiki, O'ahu

Hawai'i · year-round (summer south swell for learners) · the beachboy schools

Waikiki is the rare place where the best learner wave and the deepest heritage are the same beach. The waves are slow, soft and forgiving; the water's warm every month; and the tradition of teaching first-timers here runs straight back to Duke's beachboys. Long-standing schools work the sand daily — Hans Hedemann Surf School, founded in 1995 by the professional surfer of the same name, and Star Beachboys, a family operation with Waikiki roots going back decades. You can walk on never having touched a board and, a lesson or two later, ride the whitewater to the beach with the Duke statue watching over you.

On languageWaikiki is in the United States — instruction is in native English. You don't trade the source wave for a language barrier; here the birthplace of the modern sport is also the easiest place in the world for an English speaker to start.

What it opensSurfing gives you genuine ocean confidence — reading water, currents and your own limits — that carries to any coast. If you keep at it, the ISA (International Surfing Association) runs a real Level 1 → Level 2 instructor and coaching pathway, the recognised global standard. That's a door to teaching work, not a promised job, and lifeguarding is a separate qualification. What a first week reliably opens is a lifelong relationship with the sea.

How you'd start
  • Book a reputable Waikiki school for a group or private beginner lesson. You'll start on a big soft-top in gentle whitewater — the right way in. Confirm small groups and a real instructor, not just a board rental.
  • Learn the etiquette early. One wave, one rider; don't drop in; respect the locals and the lineup. It's the difference between being welcome in the water and being in the way.

Also real, if not the pick: Ericeira, Portugal (English widely spoken) — Europe's only World Surfing Reserve, a dense beginner-to-advanced scene along one short coast, with international camps used to teaching in English. Peniche, Portugal (English-friendly) — gentle sand at Baleal for learners and a world-stage barrel nearby once you progress. Both are excellent European options; Waikiki is the source, and the easiest English-language start of them all.
