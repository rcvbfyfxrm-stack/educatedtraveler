Tattooing

The most revered tattoo tradition on earth is one its own country made a crime — Japan outlawed irezumi for seventy-six years, and its masters kept it alive by hand, in back rooms, out of sight.

Not a weekend course. A hand-poked lineage, a guild you apprentice into, and a craft still half in the shadows. Here's what's real, what the "learn to tattoo in a week" courses sell, and how an English speaker actually begins.

What it actually is

A guild you enter, not a class you take

Every other craft on this Atlas has a certificate at the end. Tattooing has an apprenticeship — years of it — and no diploma anywhere that lets you skip it. The Japanese word is irezumi, "inserting ink," and in its oldest form it's done entirely by hand: a master stretches the skin with one hand and drives ink in with the other, using a wooden or metal rod tipped with a cluster of needles. That technique is tebori — "hand carving" — and only a small number of artists still work that way.

The maker is a horishi. The recognised ones carry a name that begins with Hori- — Horiyoshi, Horihide, Horicho — conferred inside the lineage, the way a chef earns a station. You don't award it to yourself. That single fact tells you most of what separates the real craft from what's sold: one is a name you're given after years at someone's side; the other is a kit you buy online on a Tuesday.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the ink

01

A comic book started the craze. Around 1827 the woodblock artist Utagawa Kuniyoshi published prints of the 108 heroes of the Suikoden — Chinese outlaws reimagined with dragons, tigers and waves crawling across their skin. Edo went mad for it. The city's firemen, the hikeshi, became the most heavily tattooed people in Japan, wearing water motifs as protection against the flames they ran toward.

02

Japan banned it for seventy-six years. In 1872 the modernising Meiji government outlawed decorative tattooing — an embarrassment to show the West — and it stayed illegal until 1948, lifted under the US Occupation. Three generations of masters worked as criminals. The outlaw association the ink still carries wasn't born in the craft; it was manufactured by the ban.

03

It was legally "medicine" until 2020. Even after 1948, Japanese authorities held that piercing skin with a needle was a medical act — so tattooing without a doctor's licence was a crime. An Osaka artist, Taiki Masuda, was fined, refused to pay, and fought it to the top. In September 2020 Japan's Supreme Court ruled that tattooing needs artistic skill, not a medical degree. The craft became plainly legal in living memory.

04

The bathhouse still says no. Walk into most Japanese onsen, gyms or pools with visible ink and you'll be turned away — a lingering taboo from the yakuza's adoption of irezumi and the gangster films that cemented it. Worth saying plainly: it's a taboo about an association, not a belief that every tattooed person is a criminal — and among younger Japanese it's fading.

Where it comes from

Horiyoshi III, and the hand that wouldn't quit

If irezumi has a living ambassador, it's Horiyoshi III — born Yoshihito Nakano in 1946, working out of Yokohama, where in 2000 he opened a small tattoo museum. He inherited the full classical canon — the koi, the peonies, the dragons — and the discipline behind it: much of his life's work is still done tebori, by hand, needle by needle, on skin.

He learned the machine too, in the 1980s. He simply never let the hand go.

"It was impossible to give up the tebori technique… this is where irezumi comes from. I think it's important to keep your mind on the idea that every job was at first done by hand." Horiyoshi III · interview, Big Tattoo Planet

Here's the myth worth debunking: that Japanese tattooing is, at heart, a criminal art. It isn't. It grew out of firemen and labourers and Kuniyoshi's fashion for painted heroes — ordinary people, in the open — and only went underground because the state pushed it there. The West's own encounter with it followed the craft, not the crime: in 1973, the American Ed Hardy spent months in Gifu working alongside the master Horihide (Kazuo Oguri), the first Westerner to tattoo inside the Japanese tradition — and carried its whole grammar home.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Kuniyoshi's Suikoden: Utagawa Kuniyoshi's breakout series, "The 108 Heroes of the Suikoden," began c. 1827 and drove an Edo-period tattoo fashion; the hikeshi (firemen) were among the most heavily tattooed, wearing water imagery.
  • The ban: decorative tattooing outlawed by the Meiji government in 1872; prohibition lifted in 1948 under the US Occupation — the standard cited dates.
  • 2020 ruling: Osaka artist Taiki Masuda, fined under the Medical Practitioners' Act after a 2015 raid, was cleared when Japan's Supreme Court held in September 2020 that tattooing is not a medical act and needs no doctor's licence.
  • Horiyoshi III: born Yoshihito Nakano, 1946; Yokohama-based; founded the Yokohama Tattoo Museum in 2000; still tattoos largely by hand (tebori).
  • Ed Hardy & Horihide: Don Ed Hardy worked with Horihide (Kazuo Oguri) in Gifu City in 1973 — the first Westerner to tattoo in a traditional Japanese setting.

Where it's alive now

Yokohama, Tokyo, and a tradition kept quiet

The living centre of classical irezumi is still Yokohama and Tokyo — Horiyoshi III's Yokohama base and museum, and the old horishi district of Asakusa in Tokyo, where the Edo lineage physically continued through masters and their deshi (apprentices). This is where the craft is passed hand to hand, and where the classical grammar — the way a dragon wraps a back, the way water fills the space around it — is held to its exact rules.

But be honest about the scene: it's small, and it's semi-underground. Even now, legal-but-stigmatised, the best masters keep low profiles and take students by introduction, not application. This isn't a place you drop into for a fortnight and leave a tattooer. It's a place you go to understand where the deepest version of the craft is kept — and to see, in one museum room, two centuries of it in one lineage's hands.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the con

No skill on this Atlas is sold more dishonestly than this one — because the fantasy ("learn to tattoo on your gap year") and the reality (years at a bench, drawing) look nothing alike.

✓ The craft that lasts

The apprenticeship. The real path is a deshi: years of drawing, shop labour, sterilisation, watching — earning your first needles slowly, and your name later. Ed Hardy's, Horihide's, the whole lineage — all of it runs on time served, not tuition paid.

Hygiene as the trade's spine. Bloodborne-pathogen safety, cross-contamination control, aftercare. It's unglamorous and it's the actual job. A place that teaches this first is a place that's serious.

✗ The costume that sells

"Learn to tattoo in a week." Short paid courses and mail-order kits that promise a career you can't buy. They produce "scratchers" — untrained, often unhygienic — and take real money for it.

The certificate that isn't one. No course and no purchase can license you. Only your local health authority can. Any programme implying otherwise is selling a piece of paper that doesn't open the door it names.

So the tell is simple. If someone's selling you a finished skill on a timeline — a week, a "master certificate," a kit — walk. If they're putting you on the slow road of drawing, hygiene and time at a licensed bench, that's the real one. The slow version is the only version.

One thing not to conflate
  • Sak Yant is a different tradition. The sacred bamboo-tapped tattoos of Thailand — Hindu-Buddhist protective yantras, applied by monks and ajarns, most famously at Wat Bang Phra — are their own lineage, meaning and ritual. Beautiful, and not the same craft as Japanese irezumi. Don't let anyone sell you one as the other.
  • On tebori's "magic": practitioners say hand-poking heals gentler or packs colour richer. Those claims come from studios, not evidence — so we won't state them as fact. What's true is the lineage and the discipline, not a medical superiority.

★ Our honest pick for this craft

A licensed apprenticeship — near you — with a pilgrimage to Yokohama

The bench where you live · the source in Japan

There is no plane ticket that shortcuts this. Traditional Japanese irezumi apprenticeships are near-closed to outsiders and run for years by introduction; and no destination on earth sends you home a tattooer in a week. So the real route is unglamorous and true: earn a place at a licensed studio you can get to, on the slow deshi model — drawing, hygiene, time — and go to Yokohama to stand in front of the tradition at its source (Horiyoshi III's tattoo museum, the classical work) so you know what you're aiming at.

On language & the real credentialFor an English speaker, the legitimate first step is a licensed shop apprenticeship in the US or UK, paired with the credential that actually matters legally: a Bloodborne Pathogens certification (in the US, OSHA-standard 29 CFR 1910.1030), plus first-aid where your county requires it. That certificate is real and checkable. A "tattoo diploma" is not.

What it opensA licensed foundation to practise legally where you live; fluency in a visual craft that reads in any country; and a genuine door into a lineage-based community that respects time served. Not a promised job, not an income — a skill and an entry, earned the only way this one is earned.

How you'd start
  • Build a portfolio first. Studios take apprentices on drawing, not enthusiasm. Months of drawing before you ask.
  • Get the safety cert early. Bloodborne-pathogen training is cheap, required, and signals you're serious about the part of the job that matters most.
  • Vet the shop, not the promise. Look at where former apprentices ended up and whether they're licensed and working. A school cannot license you; only a health authority can.

Also real, if not the pick: Japan — the irezumi deshi path (Japanese) — the deepest version of the craft, realistically closed to most foreigners and measured in years; go to see it, not to shortcut it. Thailand — Sak Yant (Thai) — a separate sacred tradition of monk- and ajarn-lineage, received as a blessing, not taught as a trade. Honest about both: neither is a "course" you finish in a week abroad.
