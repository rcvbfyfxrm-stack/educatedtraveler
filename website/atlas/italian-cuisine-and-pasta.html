Italian Cuisine & Pasta

In Bologna, pasta isn't a recipe — it's a measurement. The city takes it so seriously it sealed a gold tagliatella in the Chamber of Commerce to settle, for good, how wide the noodle is allowed to be.

A craft of the hands, not of secret recipes — kept alive by women who roll pasta with a metre-long pin. Here's what's real, what the jar is selling, and where an English speaker actually learns it, with a master who invites you in.

What it actually is

A craft of the hands, taught in the open

Strip away the postcard and Italian cooking is one stubborn idea: a few good ingredients, treated with respect, in the exact way a particular place has always treated them. Nowhere is that clearer than in Emilia-Romagna, the region around Bologna, where the heart of the craft is sfoglia — fresh egg pasta rolled out by hand, thin enough to read a newspaper through, with nothing but a wooden pin and a feel in the fingers that takes years to earn.

That's the thing to understand before you book anything. The skill here is not a secret recipe you could steal — the recipes are written down, some of them notarised. It's a physical craft, learned by repetition, and the people who hold it will happily show you exactly how it's done. In Bologna the pasta-maker even has a title: the sfoglina, traditionally a woman, who keeps tortellini and tagliatelle alive as living knowledge rather than a museum piece.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the noodle

01

The width of a tagliatella is a matter of public record. In 1972 the Accademia Italiana della Cucina sealed a solid-gold tagliatella in Bologna's Chamber of Commerce, fixing the cooked ribbon at exactly 8 mm — a measure that works out to one 12,270th of the height of the city's Torre degli Asinelli.

02

"Spaghetti bolognese" doesn't exist in Bologna. The meat sauce is ragù, and it's served on fresh egg tagliatelle — the flat ribbon holds it; thin round spaghetti lets it slide off — or layered into lasagne. A former mayor of the city went on national radio to say the dish the world calls "spag bol" simply isn't a thing here.

03

The ragù has an official recipe too. Notarised by the same Accademia in 1982 (and updated in 2023): coarse-ground beef, pancetta, a soffritto of carrot, celery and onion, a little milk and wine, and barely any tomato. No garlic. No herbs. What comes out of the jar is a different food.

04

The people who keep it alive have a name. The sfoglina — the hand-roller — is a recognised craft in Emilia-Romagna, and Bologna has moved to protect it, treating hand-made tortellini as a body of knowledge worth certifying, not just a nice thing grandmothers do.

Where it comes from

Alessandra Spisni, and the school for sfogline

The most valuable person in any craft isn't the most famous one — it's the master who will actually teach you. In Bologna that's Alessandra Spisni. Born in the city in 1959, a master sfoglina who became a household name rolling pasta on Italian television, she has run La Vecchia Scuola Bolognese since 1993 — widely described as the only school in the world dedicated to training sfogline, the hand-rollers who keep the tradition alive. You can walk in for a single day, or stay for the full professional course.

The myth worth puncturing is the one that says great Italian cooking is about talent or a family secret. Spisni's whole method says the opposite: it's habit, repetition, and learning to feel the dough. That is why she teaches it in the open, with her hands next to yours.

The art of the sfoglia is learned through habit and practice — you have to learn to feel it under your hands. Alessandra Spisni · Cucinare alla Bolognese

That's the honest seed of the craft, and the reason a place like this matters. The world's most celebrated kitchens are restaurants, not schools — the only way in is an unpaid, fiercely contested stage you almost never get. A master who throws the door open and puts a rolling pin in your hand is rarer, and worth crossing an ocean for.

Where it's alive now

Bologna, la Grassa

Bologna's oldest nickname is la Grassa — "the fat one" — earned over centuries as the kitchen of Italy. This is the home of tagliatelle al ragù, tortellini in brodo, lasagne verde and mortadella, and the surrounding towns of Emilia-Romagna add Parmigiano-Reggiano, prosciutto di Parma and balsamic vinegar within an hour's drive. It is, by most cooks' reckoning, the densest concentration of Italian food tradition anywhere.

What makes it a place to learn and not just eat is that the craft is still worked by hand and still taught in the open — in Spisni's school, in the tortellini a family folds together on a Sunday, in the sfogline hired to roll for a restaurant that won't cut the corner. You go to Bologna to find the pin, the board, and the people who'll tell you your dough is too dry and show you why.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the jar

Few foods on earth are as loved — and as misrepresented — as Italian. The gap between the real thing and the export version is wide, and worth naming plainly.

✓ The craft that lasts

Sfoglia by hand. Egg and flour, a wooden pin, and a feel in the fingers — fresh pasta rolled thin, cut, and filled the way a specific place does it. This is the skill that takes real teaching.

Ragù as a slow sauce, on the right pasta. Hours in the pot, little tomato, served on tagliatelle or in lasagne. Regional, precise, and nothing like a jar.

✗ The version that sells

"Spaghetti bolognese." A dish that doesn't exist in Bologna, sold worldwide as its signature — usually a tomato-heavy mince on the wrong pasta.

The 20-minute "pasta class." Handed a pre-made dough, a photo, and a limoncello. Fun; not learning. The craft is in the rolling, which is exactly the part these skip.

So the tell is simple. If a class hands you dough someone else made and rushes you to the eating, you're buying the costume. If it puts flour and eggs in front of you and makes you roll — badly at first — that's the real thing, and it's the only version that leaves you able to do it again at home. The part that's hard to teach is the part worth paying for.

The official recipes, and where to check them
  • Tagliatella: the Accademia Italiana della Cucina deposited a gold sample at Bologna's Chamber of Commerce in 1972, fixing the cooked width at 8 mm.
  • Ragù classico bolognese: recipe registered with the Chamber of Commerce in 1982 by the Accademia, revised in 2023 — coarse beef, pancetta, soffritto, milk, wine, a little tomato concentrate.
  • "Spaghetti bolognese": not a traditional Bolognese dish; the authentic pairing is tagliatelle al ragù. Bologna's former mayor Virginio Merola stated as much publicly.
  • Sources: lacucinaitaliana.com; italymagazine.com; italysegreta.com — "Spaghetti Bolognese Doesn't Exist."
Where we'd send you

Roll it by hand, in Bologna, in English

★ Enrol with the master

La Vecchia Scuola Bolognese

Bologna · Emilia-Romagna · Italy · Alessandra Spisni · day & professional courses

This is the source: the school built to keep the sfoglina tradition alive, run by the master who put it on television. The single-day class is the right first door for a traveller — a small group, a board and a pin each, and you make several kinds of pasta by hand and then sit down and eat what you rolled, with wine and bread. It's hands-on from the first minute, which is the whole point.

On languageThe day classes are taught in English as standard — visitors from all over take them, and reviewers consistently note clear, professional instruction in English. The longer professional course, aimed at people entering the trade, runs mainly in Italian.

What it opensHand-rolled pasta is a real, portable kitchen skill — it travels to a home table, a supper club, a restaurant line or a yacht galley. The day class gives you a technique you can actually repeat; the professional course is a genuine step toward working as a sfoglina or in a pro kitchen. Not a promised job — a craft people will always want, done by hand.

How you'd start
  • Book the one-day hands-on class (roughly half a day, ending in lunch). Confirm you'll be rolling your own sfoglia, not assembling a pre-made one — that's the line between a lesson and a photo op.
  • If it takes hold, ask about the professional course, or build the wider foundation at a formal school before or after. Repetition is the teacher here; one day plants it, practice keeps it.

Also real, if not the pick: ALMA, Colorno (Parma) (Italian, some English) — the international school of Italian cuisine in a ducal palace, the formal professional route if you want a full qualification. Casa Artusi, Forlimpopoli (Italian) — a living centre of Italian home cooking built around Pellegrino Artusi, where local home cooks teach; the source for the domestic tradition. Both are elsewhere in Emilia-Romagna, within reach of Bologna.
