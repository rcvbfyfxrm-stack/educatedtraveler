Where else this craft lives · ten

Listing five places would tell you this craft happens in five places, and it does not. These are the rest we know of. Nobody has checked any of them. No school here is vetted, no teacher named, nothing graded — this is the lowest state on the map, and we would rather print it than let a short list read as a complete one. A line each, and what the place is known for.

  • Kanazawa, Japan
    A castle town with its own refined kaiseki tradition and exceptional winter seafood.
  • Sapporo & Hokkaido, Japan
    The source of much of Japan's best cold-water fish, uni and crab.
  • Fukuoka, Japan
    A distinct Kyushu food culture, with yatai street stalls and its own fish market.
  • Toyama, Japan
    A deep bay producing firefly squid and white shrimp, and a strong sushi culture around it.
  • Shizuoka, Japan
    Wasabi cultivation and Suruga Bay's fish, close enough to Tokyo for a short stay.
  • Hiroshima, Japan
    Oyster country with a regional cooking identity separate from Kansai and Kanto.
  • New York, United States
    The largest concentration of serious sushi outside Japan, and the easiest to reach in English.
  • Vancouver, Canada
    A Pacific-facing city with long-established Japanese kitchens and excellent local fish.
  • São Paulo, Brazil
    The biggest Japanese diaspora anywhere, and a hybrid cuisine that came out of it.
  • Honolulu, United States
    Where Japanese technique and Hawaiian fish have been cooking together for over a century.
Is this community worth the trip

Three of the five answers are yes here

The same five questions, asked of every craft on this map, before we send anyone anywhere. A full dot is a yes we can show you the working for. An empty dot means one of three things, and the sentence under it says which: nobody publishes the answer, we read it and the answer is no, or nobody of ours has been.

●●●○○

Everything a stranger needs is here and published — teachers by name, courses in English, dates and prices — and we have still not stood at any of these counters, so the last two are ours to earn.

● Is there a teacher, and can we name them?Named, and recently better named. Tokyo Sushi Academy publishes all seven of its instructors — Fumimasa Murakami is vice-principal, and Sachiko Goto and Kumiko Imazu carry the International Sushi Course, the one taught for people who do not speak Japanese. And Hiroko Ishii, who was one of the two main instructors of that same English course, has opened her own school: JCMA in Bunkyo-ku, eight students to a room. Jiro Ono used to sit in this field on this page. He teaches at none of these schools, and taking his name out of it is what let the real ones in.

● Can a stranger get in, on a date you can book?Wide open, on dates, in English, which is rarer in this craft than the teaching. Tokyo Sushi Academy has run international courses since 2002. JCMA's diploma is six weeks in three levels — knife work and shari, then whole-fish butchery and counter service, then anago, hirame and the structure of an omakase — at 1,200,000 JPY, and they will help with the student visa. Your hands can ruin it in front of people: the final exam is serving omakase at the counter.

● Will there be other people learning beside you?Tokyo reads Legendary on the community dots above, and the evidence is the ordinary fact of the city: the market, the counters, and schools that have put thousands of foreign cooks through since 2002. There is a generation above you here and one below, and both are at work by five in the morning.

○ Is the craft alive in this place, or is the school the only thing here?We have not been. That is the whole reason this one is empty — not anything we found. This craft belongs to its place about as completely as any on the map, and one morning at Toyosu with somebody who buys there would answer it.

○ Is there enough here to keep you learning for years?The same reason. On paper the ladder is real and unusually legible — Japan has a national cooking licence, and JCMA's third level ends in a Diploma in Professional Sushi Arts rather than a certificate of attendance. We have read that. We have not watched anyone climb it, and one of these schools is three months old.

All five are reachable here: this craft belongs to its places, so one visit could answer the last two.

Sushi & Washoku — the overall

What you can realistically reach: A short intensive teaches knife handling, rice (shari) and the logic of a washoku meal — enough to cook honestly at home. The traditional path to itamae mastery is measured in years, and an honest school says so.

  • Group — Small kitchen classes, a few students per chef.
  • A normal day — Knife and rice fundamentals first, then building and plating dishes through the day; some courses include a market visit.
  • Who comes — Serious home cooks and career-changers, mixed nationalities.

This is the craft, not one school's version of it. What each place does with it is on that place's own sheet.

Why you can trust this map

How a place gets on here, and what we do when it stops being true

There is a real difference between a school that teaches you and a good-looking website, and too much of the internet is the second kind. Four instruments do the work, and every one of them is visible on the page you are already on.

  • We grade it in public, and publish what is missing. The Measure is five plain questions, not a score: is there a teacher we can name, can a stranger get in, will you be learning beside other people, is the craft alive in the place, and is there enough there to keep you going for years. An empty dot tells you which one we could not answer and why. A craft nobody has graded shows no meter at all, because an absence is not a zero.
  • We print what we checked and did NOT list. Every swept craft carries the places we looked at and turned down, each with its reason. That list is the part no marketplace prints, because a rejection is where the money is.
  • The claims are read back to their sources every night. A robot re-reads every link we publish against the school's own page each night, and every price and course name we have written a test for. page. When one stops matching three nights running, it is flagged for a human — and an entry that cannot be confirmed comes off. It is never softened.
  • Where we are not neutral, we say so above the recommendation. We run one paid week a year, with one teacher. His entry says that before it says anything good about him, and prints the way to reach him without us, at his prices.
  • Nothing here has been stood in yet. An open sheet means desk research, carefully done. No place on this map has been visited and dated by us. When one is, the check will carry a name and a date — and until then, the grade says so in the dots it leaves empty.
  • Nobody pays to be here, and nobody can pay to be left off. No commission, no listing fee, no tourist board. The order is the strength of the community, never the size of the wallet.
Before you trust any school — mine or anyone else's — ask these five things
  1. Who actually teaches it? Can you find them by name, with a track record you can check yourself?
  2. Is the craft alive in that place, or is the school the only thing there? A real scene has more than one good option.
  3. What exactly do you walk away with — a recognised qualification, or a certificate they printed themselves? Ask which.
  4. Can you speak to someone who did the course? A real person, not a testimonial on their own page.
  5. What happens on a bad day — weather, an injury, a teacher who doesn't show? A serious place has an honest answer.

If a place dodges these, that's your answer. It costs you nothing to ask, and it tells you everything.
