Mixology & Bartending

The bar's real skill was never a secret recipe. It's fresh juice, clean speed, and the read on the person in front of you — the same drink, made right, for the two-hundredth time tonight.

Not bottle-flipping, not "ancient secret art." What's the craft, what's the costume, who really invented the cocktail — and where an English speaker actually learns to work a bar.

What it actually is

Hospitality under pressure, not a bag of tricks

The first thing a good bar school beats out of you is the idea that this is about flair or secret recipes. It isn't. Strip it back and bartending is four plain things done well, fast, all night: measurement (a cocktail is a ratio, not a guess), fresh ingredients (real citrus, not a sour-mix gun), speed and consistency (the tenth round as good as the first), and — the part no course can fake — reading the room. "Mixology" is just the building side of it: balancing a drink. Bartending is doing that at pace, for real people, when it's slammed.

The anatomy is simpler than the mystique suggests. A classic cocktail is a structure — spirit, sugar, water, bitters, which is almost word-for-word the first definition anyone ever printed in 1806 — balanced across strong, sweet, sour and bitter. The tools are a jigger, a shaker, a strainer, and your hands. The craft splits in two: the canon of classics you learn cold so you can make any of them without thinking, and the drinks you build yourself once you understand why the classics work. Consistency is the real exam. Anyone can make one great Negroni. A bartender makes the same great Negroni two hundred times without looking.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the cocktail

01

The cocktail was defined in a small-town newspaper. On 13 May 1806, the editor of The Balance and Columbian Repository in Hudson, New York, answered a reader who asked what a "cock tail" was: "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters." That's the first printed definition — now marked as World Cocktail Day — and that four-part skeleton is still an Old Fashioned.

02

The first cocktail book was written by a showman. Jerry "The Professor" Thomas published How to Mix Drinks, or The Bon Vivant's Companion in 1862 — the first true bar guide in America, 500-plus recipes. His signature, the Blue Blazer, was a ribbon of flaming whisky thrown between two silver mugs in a darkened room.

03

Cuba made tending bar a licensed profession. The Club de Cantineros de Cuba (1924) was the first bartenders' guild in Latin America — exams, standards, a real trade — while most of the world still treated the bar as a stopgap job. It's where "Constante" Ribalaigua perfected the frozen daiquiri at El Floridita, blender and all.

04

The craft you drink now is barely 40 years old. It nearly died in America — Prohibition, then decades of sour mix and soda guns. The "craft cocktail" came back when Dale DeGroff put fresh citrus and forgotten classics back in the glass at New York's Rainbow Room in the late 1980s. What feels like ancient tradition is a deliberate restoration.

Where it comes from

The "secret art" that was printed the whole time

Here's the myth to put down first: that great bartending is an ancient guarded secret, passed down in whispers. It never was. Jerry Thomas wrote it all down in 1862. Harry Craddock codified 750 more at London's Savoy and published them as The Savoy Cocktail Book in 1930 — still in print. The knowledge was on the shelf. What actually happened is stranger and more honest: the craft nearly went extinct, and had to be dug back up.

The man who did the digging was Dale DeGroff. In 1987 the restaurateur Joe Baum hired him to open the bar at the Rainbow Room, high over Manhattan, and insisted on real cocktails made with fresh ingredients — nearly unheard of in the sour-mix 1980s. DeGroff went back to Thomas's book and Craddock's, reintroduced a public that had forgotten them to the Sazerac and the Ramos Gin Fizz, and squeezed actual limes. That bar reset the whole trade.

"We were a rum and Tab town then, and here we were opening a bar with no soda gun." Dale DeGroff · on opening the Rainbow Room bar

So the origin story isn't a locked recipe box — it's a revival. The classics survived in old books because someone bothered to read them; the craft survived because DeGroff, and the London and New York bartenders who followed, decided fresh and precise was worth the extra work. That's the honest lineage, and it's the opposite of a secret: everything you need is written down and pourable. The only real gatekeeper is the thousands of drinks it takes to get fast.

The receipts, date by date
  • 1806: first printed definition of "cocktail," The Balance and Columbian Repository (Hudson, NY), editor Harry Croswell answering a reader — "spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters."
  • 1862: Jerry Thomas, How to Mix Drinks, or The Bon Vivant's Companion — the first American bar guide, 500+ recipes, incl. the flaming Blue Blazer.
  • 1924: Club de Cantineros de Cuba founded — the first bartenders' guild in Latin America; Constantino "Constante" Ribalaigua's El Floridita perfects the frozen daiquiri.
  • 1930: Harry Craddock's The Savoy Cocktail Book (London) — ~750 recipes; Ada "Coley" Coleman, the Savoy's first female head bartender, had trained him.
  • 1987: Dale DeGroff opens the Rainbow Room bar for Joe Baum — fresh citrus, revived classics; widely credited with sparking the modern cocktail revival.
Where it's alive now

Two capitals and a keeper of the flame

The modern craft has two capitals, and they trade ideas constantly. London holds the American Bar at the Savoy — Britain's oldest surviving cocktail bar, open since 1893 — and, since the 2000s, one of the densest scenes of celebrated bars anywhere on earth. New York is Jerry Thomas's city and DeGroff's; it's where the revival caught fire and where the classics were written. If you want to be around people who take this seriously, one of these two is where you'll find them.

And then there's Havana — not a training capital, but the living keeper of a tradition the guild never let drop. The cantineros still work El Floridita and La Bodeguita del Medio; the daiquiri and the mojito are theirs. It's a pilgrimage more than a school, but the trade owes it a debt, and it shows you where the craft is treated as a profession with a hundred years of pride behind it.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

Few crafts on the Atlas are sold as hard, or as falsely, as this one — because "mixologist" is a word anyone can print on a certificate.

✓ The craft that lasts

Hospitality, speed, consistency. Making people feel looked after while you build the same drink perfectly, fast, for hours. That's the job, and it takes real reps.

Fresh ingredients and the classics cold. Real citrus, proper dilution, and knowing the canon well enough to riff on it. This is learnable, and it's most of the value.

✗ The costume that sells

Bottle-flipping and fire. "Flair" is a fun sport of its own, but it isn't what makes a bartender good, and no serious bar hires on it.

The weekend "mixologist" badge. A three-drink masterclass and a printed certificate teach you a party trick, not a trade. The theatre of "molecular" gadgets sells the same illusion.

The tell is simple. A real place teaches you to run a service — the speed, the consistency, the clean-down, the reading of a room — not three photogenic drinks. If a course promises to make you a "mixologist" in a weekend, it's selling the costume. The quiet, repeatable version is the valuable one.

On the most famous cocktail quote in the world — and why it's probably fake
  • The handwritten sign in La Bodeguita del Medio — "My mojito in La Bodeguita, my daiquiri in El Floridita", signed Ernest Hemingway — is one of the most reproduced lines in bar history. It is very likely a forgery.
  • A handwriting expert consulted by a Hemingway historian judged the inscription probably not his, and La Bodeguita didn't operate in its current form for much of his time in Cuba. His letters and biographers put daiquiris, wine, scotch and beer in his hand far more than mojitos.
  • It's a charming, invented trust signal — exactly the kind of thing this trade is good at selling. We'd rather tell you it's doubtful than repeat it as fact.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Floridita · Jerry Thomas (bartender) · World Cocktail Day · Ada Coleman · Harry Craddock; foxnews.com (2026) on the Hemingway mojito attribution.
Where we'd send you

Learn it where the craft was rebuilt — and in English

★ Our pick for this craft

London — the Savoy's city

England · year-round · English · European Bartender School flagship

London is where the modern craft was largely rebuilt, it's thick with celebrated bars, and it's English from the first word — so nothing is lost in translation while you learn a trade that's all about talking to people. It's also home to the flagship campus of the European Bartender School, whose four-week International Bartender Course (running since 1999) is the most established structured route in: bar practice, 65-plus classic and modern recipes, and enough reps to walk behind a bar anywhere.

On languageLondon teaches and works in English as standard. For a craft built on hospitality — reading a guest, running a room — learning it in a language you actually speak isn't a compromise, it's the point.

What it opensBartending is one of the most portable trades there is: a competent bartender can find work in almost any city, on land or on a yacht's interior, at hotel and event bars. Pair the hands-on skill with the WSET Level 3 Award in Spirits — the knowledge side, what you're actually pouring — and you carry something real. Not a promised job; a genuinely transferable skill and a credential people recognise.

How you'd start — and the honest gate
  • The gate, plainly: four weeks makes you competent to work a bar, not a master. Mastery is years of service. A course certificate is a start, not a licence or a guarantee — anyone claiming otherwise is selling.
  • The structured route: EBS's International Bartender Course (~4 weeks) covers bar practice, the classic canon, free-pour and flair basics. Confirm it's real bench time, not a demo.
  • The other route: start as a barback in a good bar and learn on the floor — slower, cheaper, and how most great bartenders actually came up. The two combine well.
  • The knowledge side: WSET Spirits (Level 1 → 3) for what's in the bottle. Separate from bartending skill, and worth having.

Also real, if not the pick: Havana, Cuba (Spanish) — the cantinero source and the El Floridita / La Bodeguita pilgrimage; heritage-rich, but arranging genuine professional training and a recognised credential as a visitor is harder. New York City (English) — Jerry Thomas's city and DeGroff's, world-class bars and schools; the American home of the revival, and every bit London's equal once you're past the basics.
