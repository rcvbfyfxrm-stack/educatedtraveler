Asado & Open-Fire Cooking

Everyone calls it Argentine barbecue. It's the opposite of barbecue — no sauce, no rub, no hurry — just salt, live coals, and a fire someone lit hours before the first plate.

The craft isn't the meat. It's the fire, and the patience to wait on it. Here's what's real, what the tourist "gaucho grill" sells, and where an English speaker actually learns to run the coals.

What it actually is

A method and a clock, not a recipe

The instinct is to treat asado as a dish — buy steak, light the grill, flip. Every real asador starts somewhere else: with fire. You burn hardwood down to embers in a side fire or a steel basket, then rake the coals under the grate and cook on steady, smokeless heat. You almost never cook over the flames. Salt is often the only seasoning that touches the meat. The chimichurri comes to the table, not the grill — it's a condiment, not a marinade, and confusing the two is the first tell of someone who learned asado from a menu.

The gear is a vocabulary. The parrilla is the grate — usually cranked up and down on a wheel to control the heat. The asador, or la cruz, is an iron cross that holds a whole animal — a lamb, a kid, a side of ribs — leaning into the fire. The brasero is where wood becomes coal. And asador is also the person: the one who runs the fire, a role of real honour and, tellingly, no certificate. It runs on time — a backyard asado is three, four, five hours, the fire lit first and fed all afternoon, the food going on in its own slow order.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the fire

01

The wood is named for breaking axes. Quebracho — the classic asado hardwood — comes from the Spanish quiebra-hacha, "axe-breaker." It's among the densest woods on earth, so heavy it sinks in water (specific gravity up to about 1.4). That's exactly why its coals burn long and hot enough to cook on for hours — the whole craft rests on embers that outlast the flame.

02

You never cook over the fire. The flame's only job is to make coals; the cooking happens over embers raked to the side or under the grate. A parrilla that's flaming is a parrilla that's failing — a rushed cook, or a spectacle put on for a table of tourists. Patience is the technique.

03

The country that worships beef is eating less of it than ever. Argentina still leads the world — roughly 47 kg (over 100 lb) a head in 2024 — but that's well down from a historical average nearer 70 kg, and by 2024, squeezed by inflation, Argentines were eating less beef than at almost any point on record. The asado endures anyway, because it was never really about the meat.

04

There are seven fires, not one. Argentina's most famous fire cook, Francis Mallmann, mapped the craft as seven distinct methods — the parrilla (grate), chapa (iron griddle), horno de barro (clay oven), rescoldo (food buried straight in embers and ash), the asador cross, the caldero (cast-iron cauldron), and the infiernillo, "little hell," with fire above and below. One craft, seven ways to put food to flame.

Where it comes from

The gaucho left no recipe — and no name

Asado has no inventor. Spanish ships brought cattle to the Río de la Plata in the 1500s; by the 1700s feral herds ran the Pampas, and the gauchos — horsemen of mixed Indigenous, Spanish and African descent — lived off them. Their method was the craft: split the ribs, drive an iron or a green stake through the carcass, plant it beside the fire angled toward the heat, and let coals and time do the rest. That's the asado al asador — a la cruz — still used for whole lamb today. As Argentina urbanised, the open pit became the parrilla, the gaucho became the asador, and the fire moved from the plains to the family backyard.

That anonymity is worth sitting with, because it's where the myths start. Asado is not "Argentine barbecue" — there's no low-and-slow smoking, no rub, no sauce mopped on the meat. And it isn't only Argentine: Uruguay makes the same claim with equal right, and in some years out-eats Argentina in beef per head. The romance of a single national founder is folklore. The real founder-figure of the craft as the world now knows it — the one who carried the gaucho's fire from the estancia into serious kitchens — is Francis Mallmann. Born in 1956 and raised in Bariloche, in the Andes of Patagonia, he trained in Paris alongside Paul Bocuse, ran fine-dining rooms in Buenos Aires, then at around forty walked away from French haute cuisine to cook the way the gauchos did. His book Seven Fires and his Chef's Table episode put Argentine live-fire cooking on tables from London to Los Angeles.

Fire is such a fragile and beautiful thing. Francis Mallmann · The Splendid Table

That fragility is the whole discipline. A fire is never finished — it builds, peaks and fades while you work — and learning to read it is the one skill no recipe can hand you. It's why the gaucho left a method and not a formula.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • Origin: the Spanish introduced cattle to the region in the 16th century; by the 1700s feral herds on the Pampas were hunted by the gauchos, whose open-fire cooking became the asado. As the country urbanised the pit gave way to the parrilla and the gaucho to the asador.
  • A la cruz / al asador: a whole animal fixed to an iron cross planted by the fire and angled toward it — the gaucho technique, still standard for whole lamb and kid.
  • Mallmann: Argentine chef, born 1956 (Acassuso, Buenos Aires Province), raised in Bariloche; trained in Paris with Paul Bocuse and others; left haute cuisine around age forty for open-fire cooking; author of Seven Fires: Grilling the Argentine Way (2009, with Peter Kaminsky) and subject of Netflix's Chef's Table (2015). His "seven fires" — parrilla, chapa, horno, rescoldo, asador, caldero, infiernillo — name the restaurant Siete Fuegos in Mendoza.
  • Not UNESCO: despite what a few tour pages imply, asado is not on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list. We'd rather tell you that than repeat a flattering claim we couldn't verify.
  • Sources: en.wikipedia.org — Francis Mallmann · Asado · Gaucho; argentineasado.com; caminopampa.com; theworlds50best.com — Siete Fuegos; splendidtable.org (quote).
Where it's alive now

A whole culture that lights a fire every week

Buenos Aires is the parrilla capital: the neighbourhood grill is an institution, the Sunday asado is a national ritual, and the argument over the right cut and the right fire is a civic sport everyone plays. Out on the Pampas estancias, the whole-animal al asador still happens over open ground, the way it always did. In Mendoza's wine country, Mallmann built Siete Fuegos at The Vines Resort in the Uco Valley — a room organised entirely around his seven fires. And across the river, Uruguay holds the craft just as tightly: Montevideo's Mercado del Puerto is a hall of open grills, one of the great live-fire rooms anywhere.

Why this matters for learning it: asado isn't a class you take once. It's something a whole society does, constantly, in the open, with opinions. You get good fastest where the fire is lit around you all the time and everyone at the table is ready to tell you what you did wrong. That is what you're really travelling for — not a certificate, but a scene.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the show

Asado splits cleanly, because the version sold to a bus of tourists and the version an Argentine family does on a Sunday barely resemble each other.

✓ The craft that lasts

Fire and patience. Reading coals, adjusting the grate's height, salting simply, cooking in order over hours. The flavour is smoke and time, not sauce.

The whole animal, nose to tail. The achuras — chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), chinchulines, and the prized mollejas (sweetbreads) — are as much the meal as the steak, and knowing their order is half the craft.

✗ The costume that sells

The "gaucho BBQ show" — flames leaping under the meat, a stopwatch, a photo op. Real asado is smokeless, slow, and undramatic to watch.

"Argentine barbecue sauce." There isn't one on the grill. Chimichurri is a table condiment; a good asador reaches for coarse salt and time, not a bottle.

So the tell is simple. If the grate is flaming and the meat is drowned in sauce, you're at a show. If it's coals, salt, and a cook who won't be hurried, you're at an asado — and it's better than the show ever was. The slow version is the real version.

On the order of the fire, plainly
  • Coals first. The fire is lit well before anything cooks; embers are moved under the grate as the food goes on, and topped up all afternoon. Never a bed of open flame.
  • Achuras open, the big cuts close. Chorizo and the offal (chinchulines quick and crisp, then morcilla, with mollejas the most prized) come early; the large cuts — vacío, tira de asado, entraña — take longest and finish the meal. Provoleta, a grilled disc of provolone, bookends it.
  • Salt, mostly. Coarse salt on the meat; chimichurri and salsa criolla on the table. Marinades and rubs are a different tradition, not this one.

Where we'd send you

Learn the fire where the whole city argues about it

★ Our pick for this craft

Buenos Aires — La Escuela Argentina de Parrilleros

Buenos Aires · Argentina · hands-on, small groups · Spanish

Argentina's first school dedicated to the parrilla, run out of the working Parrilla Checho by Carlos "Checho" López — nearly four decades at the grill, an award-winning parrilla cookbook, and more than a thousand students from around the world. The restaurant turns into a classroom in the afternoon; you light the fire, break down the cuts, and cook them in order, in small groups, with the man himself. This is the real professional route, in the city where the craft is densest.

The honest gateThe class is taught in Spanish, and there's a bigger gate worth naming: asado has no official credential. You leave able to run a fire — not with a licence, because no such licence exists. "Asador" is a reputation you earn at a hundred Sundays, not a certificate anyone can print. For a hands-on class in English, The Asado Experience (Belgrano) runs a three-hour session in a century-old family home, taught by women grill masters, from lighting the coals and choosing cuts to chimichurri — and you cook. To see it done at the top, Fogón Asado (Palermo, in the MICHELIN Guide) walks you course by course through a full asado; a meal, not a lesson, but an honest map of what "great" tastes like.

What it opensNo promised job — this is a craft, not a diploma. What it gives you is standing: you become the person who runs the fire at every gathering for the rest of your life, which in this culture is its own kind of rank. And for working cooks, live-fire technique — coal management, the chapa, the cruz — transfers straight into any grill program or open-fire kitchen, a skill in real demand as fire-cooking restaurants multiply. Not a career. A thing you can actually do, anywhere there's wood.

How you'd start
  • Book a hands-on class, not a dinner. The point is to stand at the fire yourself — lighting it, feeling the heat, timing the cuts — not to watch someone else do it.
  • Ask what you'll actually cook. A real course puts you through the order: the fire, the achuras, the big cuts, the rest. If it's a steak and a photo, it's a show.
  • Then find a Sunday. A class teaches the method; the craft is the repetition. The fastest way to get good is to keep lighting fires where people will tell you the truth about them.

Also real, if not the pick: Mendoza — Siete Fuegos by Francis Mallmann (Spanish/English, Uco Valley) — the source of the philosophy, more dinner than course, but the clearest window onto the seven fires. Montevideo & the Mercado del Puerto, Uruguay (Spanish) — asado is as Uruguayan as Argentine; the port market's wall of open grills is one of the great live-fire rooms anywhere. A Pampas estancia day (English-friendly, tourism-run) — the whole-animal al asador over open ground, if you want the gaucho picture more than the technique.
