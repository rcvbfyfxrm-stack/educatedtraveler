Bread & Boulangerie

Everyone thinks bread is a recipe — four ingredients, and you've seen all of them. The craft is the fifth ingredient nobody lists: time. France is the one country that wrote it into law.

Not a secret recipe. Flour, water, salt, a living leaven, and the patience to wait. Here's what's real, what the tourist "baking holiday" sells, and where an English speaker actually learns the craft.

What it actually is

Four ingredients, and the one that isn't on the label

Bread is flour, water, salt — and yeast or leaven to make it rise. That's the whole ingredient list, and it's why people assume it's simple. It isn't. Everything that makes a loaf worth eating happens in the fermentation: the hours the dough spends alive, digesting its own sugars, building acid and aroma and the open, chewy crumb. Rush it and you get pale, flavourless filler. Give it time — a slow levain (natural sourdough leaven) instead of a fast commercial yeast — and the same four ingredients turn into something with a taste of its own. The craft is learning to read that fermentation and get out of its way.

France takes this seriously enough to legislate it. Under the 1993 décret pain, a loaf may only be sold as pain de tradition française if it is made from wheat flour, water, salt and leaven or baker's yeast — no additives, and frozen at no stage of its making. It is one of the few places on earth where the law tells you what bread is allowed to be. That standard — plain ingredients, long fermentation, nothing hidden — is the whole craft in one sentence.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about a loaf of bread

01

France writes its bread into law — twice over. The 1993 décret pain defines what may be called traditional bread: four ingredients, no additives, never frozen. A 1998 law goes further and reserves the very word boulangerie for shops that knead, ferment and bake on the premises. Sell industrial loaves you didn't make? By law you're a dépôt de pain — a bread shop, not a bakery.

02

UNESCO protected the skill, not the object. On 30 November 2022 the "artisanal know-how and culture of baguette bread" was inscribed as Intangible Cultural Heritage — deliberately the know-how, not the loaf. Four ingredients, baked in small batches through the day, and the daily walk to the bakery that goes with it.

03

Win best baguette in Paris and you bake for the president. The Grand Prix de la Baguette de Paris hands the winner €4,000 and a year's contract supplying the Élysée Palace. And in recent years the city's best baguette has often been made by bakers who weren't born in France — the "most French" of foods, won by Parisians from everywhere.

04

The country that reveres bread is quietly eating less of it. French bread consumption is down roughly half since 1970 — from about 25 ounces a head each day after the war to under half a baguette now. Bakeries have fallen from around 54,000 in 1950 to about 32,600 today. Yet some ten billion baguettes still leave French ovens every year.

Where it comes from

Nobody knows who made the first baguette — and that's the honest part

You'll hear that Napoleon ordered long, thin loaves so his soldiers could carry them in a trouser pocket. It's a good story with no evidence behind it — no army supply record, no baking document; food historians file it under folklore. Other tales credit Vienna's steam ovens, or the Paris Métro's diggers being handed a bread they could tear instead of cut. The plainest candidate is a 1919 law that barred bakers from working between 10pm and 4am, leaving too little time for a slow overnight loaf and favouring a fast, thin, yeasted stick out of the oven by breakfast. The truth is the experts shrug: the baguette's exact origin is speculation, and the people who study it say so.

What isn't speculation is the near-death and revival of French bread in living memory. After the war, industrial shortcuts — bleached flour, additives, brutal high-speed mixing — hollowed out the taste. The man who documented the damage, and the way back, was Raymond Calvel (1913–2005), a professor at the national milling school who spent his life on why bread had lost its flavour and how to restore it. In 1974 he introduced the autolyse — a simple rest that lets flour and water bond before kneading — now used by serious bakers worldwide.

The historian who chronicled that collapse and recovery, Cornell's Steven Kaplan — twice knighted by France for his bread scholarship — built a whole book on why it mattered so much.

In a country where bread is sacrosanct, bad bread was more than a disappointment at the table — it was a threat to France's sense of itself. Steven L. Kaplan · the argument of Good Bread Is Back, Duke University Press, 2006

So the honest founder story here isn't a hero with a secret. It's a craft that declined when it was taken for granted, and came back when a professor, a historian and a generation of stubborn bakers insisted taste was worth the time. That's the version worth learning.

The receipts, claim by claim
  • The Napoleon myth: the "loaves for soldiers' pockets" order has no supporting military or baking record; historians classify it as folklore. Several rival origin stories circulate, and no one is confirmed.
  • The 1919 night-work law restricting baking between 10pm and 4am is the most-cited plausible spur to the fast, thin baguette — a contributing factor, not a proven single origin.
  • Raymond Calvel (1913–2005), of the École française de meunerie, authored Le Goût du Pain (1990; English: The Taste of Bread) and introduced the autolyse method in 1974; he is a standard authority on French breadmaking.

Where it's alive now

Paris argues about bread the way other cities argue about football

Bread is most alive where people care loudly about it, and nowhere cares louder than Paris. The city runs an annual contest to crown its best baguette; bakers talk crust and crumb the way sommeliers talk vintages; and the daily walk to the corner boulangerie is a social habit UNESCO went out of its way to protect. This is the scene where you learn what "good" even means — because everyone around you has an opinion and isn't shy about it.

It's also where the other tradition lives — not the baguette but the levain loaf. Poilâne, founded on the rue du Cherche-Midi in 1932 and now run by Apollonia Poilâne, its third generation, never chased the fashionable white stick: it built its name on a round, two-kilo sourdough miche from stone-ground flour and a wood-fired oven. That sourdough lineage is the one the modern craft has run with — and it has spread out from Paris into the countryside and the wider world, which is exactly where the best hands-on teaching now is.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

Bread splits cleanly, because the thing that takes years to learn and the thing sold to visitors in an afternoon look nothing alike.

✓ The craft that lasts

Fermentation and time. Building and reading a levain, judging a dough by feel, letting a slow proof do the work. This is the part that transfers to any flour, any oven, your own kitchen.

The plain standard. Four honest ingredients, nothing hidden, nothing frozen — the tradition bar. Learn to hit it and you can taste when someone hasn't.

✗ The costume that sells

The "ancient secret recipe." There isn't one. The romance — Napoleon's pockets, a lost family formula — sells tours; the fermentation is what makes bread, and it's not a secret.

Warm lighting over frozen dough. Par-baked industrial loaves finished on-site under the word "artisan," and one-day "French baking holidays" that are a demo and a photo, not training.

Here's the con, said plainly. In France the words boulangerie and boulanger are protected by law — you may only use them if the bread is kneaded, fermented and baked where it's sold, never frozen. Cross the border and that protection vanishes. A tourist "artisan bakery experience" abroad can borrow a word the French courts would deny it. The test is simple: is the dough made and baked in front of you, over hours — or delivered and warmed?

On the law, and the words, plainly
  • Décret n°93-1074 (13 September 1993) — "pain de tradition française" must be wheat flour, water, salt and leaven/baker's yeast only; no additives; not frozen at any stage of production.
  • The 1998 law reserves "boulangerie"/"boulanger" for businesses that select the flour and knead, ferment and bake on the premises; a shop selling bread it didn't make on-site is a "dépôt de pain," not a bakery.
  • Neither travels. Both are French statutes. Outside France there's no legal floor under the word "artisan bakery," so judge the process, not the sign.
  • Sources: legifrance.gouv.fr — décret n°93-1074 · boulangerie.org — pain de tradition française · the 1998 "boulangerie" designation.
Where we'd send you

Learn the levain where it's actually taught

★ Our pick for this craft

École Internationale de Boulangerie

Noyers-sur-Jabron · Vallée du Jabron, Provence · France · natural leaven & organic flour

Founded in 2005 by Thomas Teffri-Chambelland, a scientist-turned-baker, the school sits in a Provençal village and teaches one thing seriously: bread made with natural leaven and organic flour. It takes around 200 trainees a year, roughly forty of them on the long qualifying track — many of whom go on to open their own sourdough bakeries. It's a working, hands-in-the-dough school, not a demo kitchen, and it runs specific courses in English. For learning the levain craft at its source, this is the one we'd send you to.

On languageThe core professional diploma is taught in French, but the school offers named courses in English — including its advanced sourdough, viennoiserie and panettone courses. If you speak only English, you can still train here; check which sessions are English-run before you book.

What it opensThe school's diploma is a French state-registered professional qualification — enough, on its own, to set up a bakery in France without holding the separate CAP. Short courses build real skill rather than a diploma. What it opens is honest and concrete: the confidence and craft to bake seriously, and for those who go the distance, a genuine path to opening a bakery. Not a promised job — a skill you keep.

How you'd start
  • Pick the course to the goal. A short English sourdough or viennoiserie course is days of intensive hands-on work — technique, not a certificate. The qualifying diploma track is months and aimed at people planning to open a bakery.
  • Know what the paper is. France's baseline trade credential is the state CAP Boulanger (Éducation Nationale). A private school's diploma counts professionally only when it's registered with France Compétences — this school's is. Ask which any course leads to.

Also real, if not the pick: Le Cordon Bleu, Paris — Boulangerie Diploma (taught with English translation) — the well-known institutional route, in the capital. INBP, Rouen & EBP, Paris (French) — the reference professional schools and the standard path to the state CAP Boulanger. The San Francisco Baking Institute (English, USA) — excellent levain teaching closer to home if you're US-based, though it's the craft abroad, not at its source.
