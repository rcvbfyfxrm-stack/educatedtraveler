Wine & Sommellerie

Wine's most famous title went to twenty-four people in a single week — and was taken back from twenty-three. The craft underneath is real. Most of what's built on top of it is theatre.

What's actually worth learning, what the pin is really worth, and where an English speaker learns this at the source instead of in a classroom two countries away.

What it actually is

Ninety per cent of the job happens before anyone tastes anything

The image is a person swirling a glass and naming a village. The job is mostly logistics. A working wine professional buys — negotiating, forecasting, deciding what a room will actually drink. They manage a cellar: what's stored where, at what temperature, what's ready, what's dying, what's tying up money on a shelf. They write a list that has to make sense and make margin. And then, at service, they read a table in about eight seconds and put the right bottle in front of it.

The tasting matters — but not the way it's sold. Blind tasting is a training tool: a way to force you to describe what's in the glass without the label doing your thinking. It became the sport because it's the only part that's watchable. Nobody films a stock take.

The genuinely hard skill underneath is unglamorous: calibration. Learning that what you call "high acid" is what everyone else calls high acid. That's why tasting with other people, repeatedly, in the same room, beats tasting alone with a book — and it's most of the argument for learning somewhere the wine is actually made.

The part that surprises everyone

Four things nobody tells you about the wine world

01

The word means "beast of burden." Sommelier comes through Old French from a word for the person in charge of the pack animals and the baggage train — the one who moved the load. It's a cousin of "sumpter" and, oddly, of "summer" in its old sense of a load-bearing beam, all tracing back to Latin sagma and Greek sattein, "to pack." The most rarefied job in hospitality started out as the staff who handled the luggage.

02

You could fit every Master Sommelier on earth into one small wedding. As of July 2026 there are 299 in the world. The Master of Wine — a different qualification entirely, and constantly confused with it — numbers 422 active MWs across 30 countries as of February 2026. Two titles, two organisations, and most people outside the trade think they're the same thing.

03

The most famous exam in wine was voided by a two-word email. September 2018, Four Seasons St Louis: 141 candidates, and a record 24 passed. Then it emerged that a board member and exam proctor had emailed at least three candidates before the blind flight with the subject line "Heads up" and the wines abbreviated. The Court of Master Sommeliers Americas board voted to suspend the titles of 23 of the 24. Of the 21 later offered a free re-sit that December, six passed.

04

And that wasn't the real reckoning. In 2020 a New York Times investigation exposed a culture of sexual harassment inside the same organisation. The entire board resigned ahead of that year's elections, and a co-founder and a former vice-president were later expelled following investigation. If you are deciding whether to chase a title, that history is part of the honest picture — and almost nobody selling you a course will mention it.

Where it comes from

From the baggage train to the pin

The trade grew upward. The person who guarded what travelled became the person who guarded what was stored, then the person who chose it, then — much later — an authority with an examination behind them. The modern prestige system is young: the Institute of Masters of Wine and the Court of Master Sommeliers are twentieth-century inventions, and the Wine & Spirit Education Trust was founded in London in 1969 to serve the UK trade before it became the global standard it is now.

The inflated story is that these titles measure a rare, almost supernatural palate. What they actually measure is study, service technique, and the ability to perform under a clock. That's a real achievement. It is not sorcery — and the 2018 exam is the clearest proof, because the thing that broke it wasn't a superior palate. It was two abbreviations.

Heads up — PG, CndP. The leaked email, September 2018 — Pinot Gris and Châteauneuf-du-Pape, two of the six wines. It cost 23 people the title.

Read that and the hierarchy looks different. Not fake — the six-wines-in-twenty-five-minutes exam is genuinely brutal, and the people who pass it clean have done something hard. But a system that can be undone by a text message is a system, not a priesthood. Treat the letters after a name as information, not as proof.

Where it's alive now

The qualification lives in a city. The wine doesn't.

This is the split that decides where we'd send you. The credential economy is centred on London and a handful of exam cities — that's where the bodies are, where the syllabi are written, where you sit the paper. The craft lives in the places that make the stuff: Bordeaux, Burgundy, Rioja, Piedmont, the Douro, Mosel, Barossa, Marlborough.

You can get the letters without ever standing in a vineyard in October. Plenty do. And it shows — in a very specific way. Somebody who has only studied wine can describe a region; somebody who has been there in harvest knows why the wine tastes like that, because they have seen the slope, the soil, the fog that comes off the river at six in the morning, and the grower deciding whether to pick today or gamble on three more days of sun.

Bordeaux is the clearest example of a place that decided to teach. The Bordeaux Wine School was set up by the region's own wine council in 1989 and now puts something like 80,000 people a year through its courses — professionals and enthusiasts — in French, English, Spanish and Chinese. That is not a boutique operation. It's a whole region running its own education, at the source, in a language you speak.

The honest read

What's the craft, and what's the costume

No skill on the Atlas has a wider gap between the thing and the marketing of the thing. Wine sells mystique because mystique carries a margin.

✓ The craft that lasts

Service. Opening cleanly, decanting when it helps and not when it doesn't, temperature, glassware, pacing a table. Unshowy, immediately visible, and the first thing a good employer checks.

Buying and matching. Choosing what a specific room will drink, at a price that works, and putting the right thing in front of the right person.

Calibrated tasting. Describing what's in the glass in language other people share — built by tasting with others, repeatedly.

✗ The costume that sells

The pin. Real information about study completed. Not a measure of palate, and — see 2018 — not incorruptible.

Blind tasting as a party trick. A training tool turned into a spectator sport because it's the only photogenic part of the job.

Scores and mystique. A number out of 100 is one person's opinion wearing a lab coat. Useful as a signal, useless as a truth.

The con, where there is one, is a school selling you the exam rather than the wine. The tell is simple: ask how many hours you'll spend tasting with other people in the room, and how many bottles you'll actually open. A course that can be passed by reading is a course about an exam.

Where we'd send you

Two things worth having, and they aren't the same thing

Most guides pick one and pretend the other doesn't matter. For anyone working — and especially for crew — you want both, and it helps to be clear about which is doing what.

★ The knowledge that outlasts the pin

École du Vin de Bordeaux — the Bordeaux Wine School

Bordeaux · France · founded 1989 by the CIVB · courses in English

Our source-first pick, and the reason is our whole method: learn a region in the region. Set up by the Bordeaux wine council itself, the school teaches roughly 80,000 people a year, runs in English alongside French, Spanish and Chinese, and — the part that matters if this is your trade — builds tailor-made programmes for the wine trade and for hospitality, not just visitor tastings. You are learning claret in the place that argues about claret for a living.

On languageEnglish is a standard option here, not a favour — the school teaches in four languages and has done for decades. Confirm the language of your specific course when you book; the tasting workshops in particular are scheduled per language.

What it opensDepth you can't fake at a table. For crew: the confidence to build and defend a wine list, to buy sensibly in a French or Spanish port instead of over-paying for labels you recognise, and to talk about a bottle without either bluffing or apologising. It's not a certificate — see the second pick for that. It's the part that makes the certificate worth something.

How you'd start
  • Start at bordeaux.com/en/bordeaux-wine-school — the CIVB's own English page, with the course range and booking.
  • Say you're in the trade when you enquire. The tailored professional programmes are a different animal from the tourist workshop, and they don't always sit on the front page.
  • Go in harvest if you can — late summer into October. The region is at its least relaxed and its most honest.
  • We quote no price here because the professional programmes are quoted per programme. Ask, and treat any figure as a starting point, never a checkout.
★ The credential that actually gets read

WSET Level 2, then Level 3 — taken in a wine region

Wine & Spirit Education Trust · founded London 1969 · 70+ countries · 800+ approved providers

If a certificate is going on your CV, this is the one the industry reads. Level 2 is the sensible working qualification; Level 3 is a genuine step up in both study and tasting. The useful trick, and the reason it belongs on this page rather than a London one: WSET is delivered by more than 800 approved providers in over 70 countries — so you can sit exactly the same qualification with a provider based in a wine region, taught by people who work in it. Same certificate, better tasting room, better company.

The gate, shown straightLevel 3 has a written paper and a blind tasting component, and it is a real fail-able exam — plan to study, not just attend. And be clear about what it is: a qualification, not a job, and not a claim about your palate. Anyone telling you WSET certification leads to a specific role is selling.

What it opensThis is the honest yacht answer. Wine appreciation sits inside standard superyacht interior training, and a WSET line on a CV is one of the few wine credentials a chief stew or a captain will actually recognise. It travels: restaurants, retail, distribution, and the land job later. It is a door-opener, never a promise — no honest person guarantees you a position.

Also real, if not the pick: the Court of Master Sommeliers — the service-and-somm track, strong on floor craft; read the section above before you commit to that ladder. The Institute of Masters of Wine — the academic summit, years long, 422 people worldwide; not a starting point. École des Vins de Bourgogne (Burgundy, English available) and Rioja's and Piedmont's regional schools — the same source-first logic as Bordeaux, in a different grape.

One thing we won't pretend: this craft has no single named master to apprentice under, the way pottery has Mashiko or knife-making has a forge. Wine's teaching is institutional — councils, trusts, schools. We'd rather say that plainly than invent a guru for the shape of the page.
