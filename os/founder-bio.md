## Full Story

### I. The Mountains

I grew up in the Jura, the mountains of France north of the Alps—about as far from the sea as you can get in this country.

I finished a scientific baccalauréate—science A-levels—and left at eighteen, because I wanted to know what the world was really about, not what I'd been told it was about. The books did it: Thoreau, Jack London, Byron, and the navigators—Kersauson, Moitessier. I wanted two things: to travel, and to learn English. So I looked up where the Anglo-Saxon world lived and landed on Australia—big space, the far side of the planet, English, and a year on a working holiday visa. That was enough.

I worked factory shifts to get there—Président, Comté, Barry Callebaut chocolate, a wood workshop—anything that paid, to sweat and save. Five months later I flew to Melbourne with about two thousand euros.

### II. Australia

I hitchhiked the coast from Melbourne to Sydney, then ran the whole loop—Adelaide, Esperance, Perth, Fremantle, up to Broome. When the money ran out, I took whatever work moved me forward: a crocodile abattoir, building market stands, milling dirt in a mine forty hours a week, Domino's, Red Rooster. A short sacrifice each time, for more freedom after.

The kitchen found me in Darwin. I was washing dishes in a Mexican restaurant, and when the chef was short-handed after lunch he'd put me on prep and teach me my first knife skills. Hands-on, beside someone who knew his craft, in the right place for it. That's how it started—not in a book.

Australia is where I started sailing, too. First classes in Sydney Bay, in Manly, and a spot on regatta crews just by asking at the dock if a captain needed an extra hand.

That's how a man from the Jura—the furthest place from the sea in France—learned to cook and to sail.

### III. The Beginning

Then came a Day Skipper, zero-to-hero course in Thailand. Classroom to helm in weeks—my first real certification. The STCW course ran right after, the one ticket you need to work at sea. That's when I realised there was a whole industry called yachting, where you could get paid to sail around the world.

### IV. The First Yacht

My first job was on a CNB 26 with a Danish owner-captain—I was something like his second. He did everything himself, and anything to be fixed or built from scratch seemed easy to him. I learned an enormous amount working alongside him: how to troubleshoot an engine, read weather, make decisions when things go wrong. I cooked a lot on that boat, and the family loved it—which pushed me to do more.

That's where I understood: the best learning happens beside someone who's done it for years.

### V. The Galley

Parallel to sailing, I developed my other passion: cooking.

I started focusing on yacht cooking—provisioning in foreign ports, creating menus that worked with sea state and limited galley space, feeding crews and guests who'd eaten everywhere. The galley became my second classroom.

There were deliveries too—Lagoons from the factory in Les Sables d'Olonne to charter companies in Croatia. Week after week, mile after mile, until passage-making became second nature.

### VI. The Megayacht

After the Danish boat I joined a 20-metre catamaran for an Australian family with an Australian captain—a really cool family vibe. I did everything: drove the tender, cleaned the cabins, cooked. We ate together, went to restaurants together. One day, anchored off Formentera, they pointed at a megayacht—Symphony—and said, "One day you'll work on that."

That's where the first megayacht opportunity came from. I got the position of crew chef on the MY Symphony.

### VII. The Star

I decided it was time to take the cooking further. Working the Med, I looked up the Michelin-starred restaurants in France that cooked the Mediterranean, and wrote a single email to Le Petit Nice in Marseille, telling them what I was doing and where I came from.

A couple of weeks later, a phone call. They were opening a new restaurant near Aix-en-Provence, aiming for the stars. The chef there—a hunter, on land and at sea—loved my profile and took me under his wing. Within a month I was chef de partie. We earned a Michelin star six months after opening. I stayed about a year, and then it was time to go back to the sea.

### VIII. The Love That Never Stopped

The love for sailing never stopped. Every time I had time off, I'd join boats for new adventures.

I took a season as chef on a 30m sailboat, then crossed the Atlantic with a 70-year-old Scotsman on a 55ft Amel—three months from Spain to Martinique by way of Morocco, the Canaries and Cape Verde. One night on watch, in the immensity of the dark, I saw light near the hull: two dolphins playing in bioluminescent plankton, each carving its own tunnel of light. It was like a dream.

Then I joined a bluewater ocean racer—an 80ft Farr monohull, the Weddell—looking for crew. I met them in Cape Town and we crossed to Antigua, stopping at St Helena. Spartan conditions: a one-square-metre fridge for thirteen, no shower, buckets of salt water, rationed tuna and tortillas. We raced Antigua Sailing Week, the Classic Week on a Dragon, then the Antigua–Bermuda Race and sailed her back to France.

Ten years. Thousands of miles. Every ocean teaching something different. Along the way I kept learning new skills—freediving, windsurfing, trekking, breathwork.

### IX. The Schooner

Now I've bought my own sailboat—an old schooner from 1979 that I'm restoring in Barcelona.

She needs work. But she's mine. And every hour I spend fixing her, I think about the journey that led here.

### X. Why EducatedTraveler

I'm building EducatedTraveler to help people like me live the life they want to live.

I believe we all share something in common: we wanted an adventurous life.

My hope is to connect all of us together—the dreamers, the sailors, the schools that make it possible.

The masters exist—but they don't advertise. The places exist—but logistics overwhelm people. The transformation is real—but friction stops them.

So I built the bridge.

- **Real certifications**: RYA, PADI, WSET, Yoga Alliance—credentials that open doors worldwide
- **The actual source**: Gibraltar straits. Thai islands. Mediterranean passages.
- **Masters who teach**: Decades of expertise, not social media followers
- **Cohorts who bond**: 8-12 people who become friends through shared challenge

What took me a decade, you can experience in weeks or months.

### XI. Your Turn

The world is full of skills waiting to be learned, places waiting to be experienced, masters waiting to teach.

The only thing standing between you and that version of yourself is the decision to go.

**Your adventure is waiting where it's always been: at the source.**
